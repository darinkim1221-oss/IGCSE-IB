Scarcity (primary)Choice (primary)Efficiency (primary)
This lesson is the entry point to IB Unit 1 (Introduction) and IGCSE Topic 1 simultaneously. Everything downstream in the spine — demand/supply, elasticity, market failure, and eventually macro and global modules — is a restatement of "scarcity forces choice under some allocation mechanism." Treat this lesson as installing the lens students will use in every subsequent lesson, not as a stand-alone topic to be checked off.
Detailed Teaching Notes
Precision first, then structure
Unlike Tier 1, do not open with pure discovery — open with the same hook question ("could even the richest country have everything it wants?") but move quickly to precision: this is the lesson where students learn that economics rewards exact, testable definitions over vague gestures. Run the command-term drill early (slide s1): show the same concept (scarcity) phrased as a Define, State, and Describe question, and have students notice how the expected answer length and depth changes with the command word. This is a technique they will reuse in every future lesson's Exam Adapter Slot.
For opportunity cost, insist students name a specific stakeholder each time (slide s2's four tabs: consumers / workers / firms / governments) — IGCSE and IB examiners consistently penalise vague opportunity-cost answers that don't specify whose cost it is and what, precisely, is foregone.
The PPC is the load-bearing diagram of this lesson (and the wiki's §8 bridge shows it gets promoted directly into growth analysis at M2L4 and IB LRAS). Insist on the exam-standard labelling discipline from day one: axes labelled with units/goods named, curve bowed outward (concave to origin — do NOT let students draw a straight line at this tier, unlike the Tier 1 trade-off curve), points lettered, and — critically — the vocabulary split: a movement along the curve represents a choice/opportunity cost between two attainable points; a shift of the curve represents economic growth (or contraction). This distinction is examined constantly and is the single most common source of lost marks at this level.
Economic systems: push past mere description into the exam-required arguments for and against each system (IGCSE 2.8/2.10). Use the country-chip interactive (US / North Korea / Singapore) to force students to place real economies on the market–planned spectrum rather than treating the three types as purely theoretical.
The circular flow model (slide s6) is explicitly IB enrichment scaffolding — it is not in the IGCSE 0455 syllabus. Flag this openly to mixed classes: IGCSE-track students should still learn it (it makes M2 macro much easier later) but should know it will not appear on their exam.
Close with positive vs normative — the seed for evaluative writing across the whole course. The test to drill is testability, not truth: "Can this statement, in principle, be checked against evidence?" not "Do I agree with it?"
Visuals & Diagrams
📉
PPC: always draw bowed outward (increasing opportunity cost as you move along it — resources aren't perfectly substitutable between the two goods). Label axes with actual goods (e.g. Consumer Goods / Capital Goods), not just "Good X / Good Y," for exam-realistic practice. Mark A (on curve, efficient), B (inside, underutilised/unemployed resources), C (outside, unattainable with current resources).
🔄
Circular flow (2-sector): two boxes (Households, Firms), factor market flow at top (factors of production → households; income → firms... reversed correctly: households supply factors to firms, firms pay income to households) and goods market flow at bottom (firms supply goods/services to households; households pay expenditure to firms). Use arrows, not just boxes — direction is often the examined detail.
Tip: when a student draws the PPC as a straight line (carried over from a Tier 1 background), don't just correct it — ask "why would giving up the LAST unit of capital goods to get one more consumer good cost more than giving up the first unit?" and let them reason toward increasing opportunity cost themselves.
Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "A government has a fixed budget. It can spend it on healthcare or on defence. It chooses to increase defence spending by $2 billion." Model a full AO2 answer: (1) name the opportunity cost precisely — the healthcare provision foregone, not just "money"; (2) name a specific affected stakeholder group — e.g. patients on waiting lists; (3) note this is a movement, not a shift — the country's total productive capacity hasn't changed, only the allocation within it.
Common Misconceptions
"Opportunity cost is the money paid." Fix: money is the measuring tool; the true cost is always the next-best foregone alternative — drill this with a zero-cost example (e.g. free time) where there's no money involved at all.
"A shift of the PPC and a movement along it are basically the same thing." Fix: use the two-question test — "Has the economy's total capacity changed (shift) or just the allocation within the same capacity (movement)?"
"Positive = correct, normative = wrong." Fix: give a positive statement that is actually false ("Inflation in this country is currently 50%" — testable, and could just be wrong) to show positive ≠ true, just testable.
Exam Adapter Slot
Source: Wiki §7 drip map, row M1 L1 ("Foundations")
Skill focus this lesson: precise definitions; PPC & circular flow drawn and labelled to exam standard. Command terms in play: define, state, list, describe.
IGCSE (AO1)
1-sentence Cambridge command-word definitions; diagram labelling discipline.
Practice move: give students a definition with one word wrong or vague and have them correct it to Cambridge-exact phrasing.
IB (AO1)
2–3 sentence IB-style full definitions; first 'explain' cause-chain.
Practice move: extend every IGCSE 1-sentence definition into IB form by adding "...which means that..." and one consequence.
Note for mixed classes: this is the first lesson where the two adapters visibly diverge in expected answer length — use it to set expectations for the rest of the course rather than treating it as a one-off.
Extension Activities
  • Real economy audit: research one real country not covered in class and place it on the market–planned spectrum with 2 pieces of evidence.
  • PPC construction: given a small dataset (e.g. 5 combinations of two goods a hypothetical economy can produce), plot an actual PPC and identify which points are efficient.
  • Positive/normative hunt: find 3 statements from a real news article and classify each, justifying the harder judgement calls.

    Exam-Precise Glossary
    ScarcityThe condition where finite resources are insufficient to satisfy infinite/unlimited wants.
    Opportunity costThe value of the next-best alternative foregone when a choice is made.
    Factors of productionLand, labour, capital, enterprise — the inputs combined to produce goods and services; rewarded by rent, wages, interest, and profit respectively.
    PPC (Production Possibilities Curve)A diagram showing the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can produce with fixed resources and technology.
    Economic systemThe mechanism by which a society answers what, how, and for whom to produce (market, planned, or mixed).
    Positive statementA testable/verifiable claim about what is — can in principle be checked against evidence.
    Normative statementA value judgement about what ought to be — cannot be settled by evidence alone.
    Circular flow of income[IB enrichment] A model showing the flow of factors/income and goods/expenditure between households and firms.
    Key Takeaways
    1. Scarcity is the universal starting condition of economics — no system, however designed, eliminates it. 2. Every choice carries an opportunity cost that must be named precisely, by stakeholder. 3. The PPC formalises trade-offs and growth: movement along = opportunity cost, shift = growth. 4. Economic systems differ in HOW they answer what/how/for whom, each with real trade-offs — none is exam-correct by default. 5. Positive vs normative is a test of testability, not truth — the seed of all future evaluative writing.
