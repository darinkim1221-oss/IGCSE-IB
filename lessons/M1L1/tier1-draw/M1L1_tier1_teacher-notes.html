Learning Objectives
By the end, I can…
explain scarcity in my own words using a real example from my life
describe opportunity cost as "the next-best thing I gave up"
name the four factors of production and match each to its reward
read a simple trade-off curve and explain what moving along it means
compare a market, planned, and mixed village and say who decides what gets made in each
tell the difference between a fact and an opinion
Key Concepts & the IB Lens
Scarcity (primary)Choice (primary)Efficiency (primary)
This is the foundation lesson of the entire spine — every later lesson (demand & supply, elasticity, firms, even macro and global modules) is a variation on "scarcity forces choice." Plant the phrase early and return to it: whenever a later lesson feels hard, you can always ask "where's the scarcity, and what's the opportunity cost here?"
Detailed Teaching Notes
Story → Discovery → Naming
Open cold, no definitions: "Could even the richest country on Earth have everything it wants?" Let the class argue both sides for 2 minutes before revealing anything — the disagreement itself is the hook. Most 10–12 year-olds will initially say "yes, if they had enough money," which is exactly the misconception this lesson is built to dismantle.
Move straight into the desert island raft game (embedded in the slide deck, and a fuller version in the standalone "Island Traders" game file). The physical constraint — only 3 or 4 items fit — does the teaching for you. Resist the urge to define scarcity before they've felt the raft filling up. Let a student say it in their own words first, then supply the formal term: finite resources vs. infinite wants.
The "give up to get" trading round (slide s4) makes opportunity cost physical rather than abstract. The critical teaching beat is the phrase after each choice: "You got [X] — so what did you give up?" Say this out loud every single time a student makes a choice anywhere in the lesson (even off-topic ones, like choosing where to sit) to build the reflex.
For factors of production, use a concrete local example the students recognise — a school bake sale works well: land = the table space, labour = the students baking, capital = the oven, enterprise = whoever had the idea and takes the risk if it flops. The flip-card game in the deck reinforces reward vocabulary (rent/wages/interest/profit) — don't over-drill the terms; recognition is the Tier 1 bar, not recall under pressure.
The pizza-vs-robots trade-off curve is deliberately a straight line, not a bowed PPC — do not introduce "increasing opportunity cost" or curve concavity at Tier 1; that promotion happens at Tier 2. The point here is only: more of one thing (along a fixed-size raft/budget/factory) means less of the other.
The three villages vote works best as a real show-of-hands vote before revealing each other's reasons — public commitment makes the later "defend your choice in one sentence" much livelier. Do not present any village as objectively "correct" — the point is that reasonable people can choose differently, which seeds positive vs. normative thinking.
Visuals & Diagrams
🏝️
Raft sketch: on the whiteboard, draw a simple raft with exactly 3–4 boxes on it before the digital game starts. Physically cross out boxes as the class "fills" a raft together as a warm-up — the tactile version primes the digital one.
📈
Trade-off curve: sketch it freehand as a straight diagonal line, NOT a bowed curve — label only the two axis ends (e.g. "0 robots / 10 pizza" to "10 robots / 0 pizza"). Common student mistake: assuming the line must curve because "that's what graphs look like" — reassure them a straight trade-off is fine at this stage.
Tip: if a student asks about the "real" curved PPC, praise the curiosity and simply say "Tier 2 economists draw it as a curve, for a reason you'll discover later — for now, straight is fine."
Worked Example
Story: "Mia has $10 and wants both a $10 book and a $10 video game. She can only buy one. She buys the book. What's her opportunity cost?" Walk the class to the answer together: the opportunity cost is the video game — not the $10 (the money is just the tool used to make the choice; the opportunity cost is always the next-best alternative given up).
Common Misconceptions
"Scarcity just means poor." Fix: point out that even a billionaire has only 24 hours a day — time is scarce for everyone, rich or poor.
"Opportunity cost = the money spent." Fix: always ask "but what did you NOT get?" — the cost is the foregone alternative, not the price tag.
"More money = no more scarcity." Fix: use the "richest country" hook question again as a callback — wants grow as resources grow.
Think Like an Economist (5 min, replaces the exam-adapter slot at Tier 1)
Close with the reflection routine, not exam content: What did we discover today? (scarcity forces choice; every choice has a cost) → Where else does this happen? (ask 2–3 students for an example from outside class — sports team selection, limited weekend time, a shared family car).
Extension Activities
  • Article hunt (mission-formatted): find one news headline this week about something being "in short supply" — bring it in and identify what's scarce and who's affected.
  • Mini debate: "Should video game time be rationed by parents, like a scarce resource?" — 2 min per side, no research needed.
  • Family mission: the workbook's take-home opportunity-cost interview (see Homework Workbook).

    Child-Friendly Glossary
    ScarcityNot having enough of something for everyone who wants it.
    Opportunity costThe next-best thing you give up when you make a choice.
    Factors of productionThe four ingredients needed to make anything: land, labour, capital, enterprise.
    Economic systemThe way a group decides what gets made, how, and who gets it.
    FactSomething that can be checked and proven true.
    OpinionSomeone's feeling or judgement — it can't be proven right or wrong.
    Key Takeaways
    1. Scarcity is universal — it never fully disappears, even for the richest. 2. Every choice has a hidden price: the next-best alternative you gave up. 3. Four factors (land, labour, capital, enterprise) combine to make everything, each earning its own reward. 4. Different economic systems answer "what/how/for whom" differently — none is automatically "correct."
