Detailed Teaching Notes
Full syllabus rigor — the diagrams, the causes, the policy evaluation
  • Command-term drill: run Define / Explain / Evaluate first — "evaluate" previews the policy-limitation reasoning needed at the end of the lesson.
  • Population definitions & optimum population tabs: cover birth/death/net migration rate causes, effects of population size change, and the "optimum population" idea (a population size that maximises output per person, given resources) as a bridge concept for IB SL.
  • Population pyramid diagram: drill the sequence — identify which end of the pyramid narrows/widens, THEN state the demographic cause (falling birth rate, rising life expectancy), THEN state the economic consequence (dependency ratio, pension/healthcare funding pressure).
  • Poverty definitions & causes tabs: keep absolute vs relative sharply distinguished — a common exam error is treating them as synonyms. Causes should link explicitly to the international-differences content (productivity, sector composition, education, healthcare, natural resources).
  • Lorenz curve & Gini coefficient: drill reading the diagram — the FURTHER the curve bows from the 45° diagonal, the HIGHER the Gini coefficient and the MORE unequal the income distribution. This is the direct Tier-2 formalisation of the Tier-1 Candy Economy activity.
  • Redistribution policy tabs: six policies (progressive taxation, benefits/transfers, minimum wage, free education, universal healthcare, subsidised housing) each with mechanism AND at least one limitation — this is deliberately structured to feed directly into the Q6 evaluation essay.
  • IB HL enrichment: flag clearly that the market's structural inability to achieve equity on its own, and gender/population policy debates, are IB HL extension content — not required at IGCSE 0455.
Visuals & Diagrams
📊
Two key diagrams anchor this lesson. The population pyramid (young vs ageing toggle) makes demographic transition visible; the Lorenz curve (more-equal vs less-equal toggle, approximate Gini values 0.28 and 0.52) makes income inequality visible and measurable. Students must be able to read BOTH diagrams and connect them to real consequences, not just recognise their shapes.
Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "Discuss whether progressive taxation and government benefits are an effective way to reduce income inequality." Model: CLAIM — redistribution can meaningfully narrow inequality but has real limits. EVIDENCE — progressive taxation takes a larger share of income from high earners while transfers raise the income of low earners, moving the Lorenz curve closer to the diagonal; however, very high tax rates can reduce incentives to work or invest, and poorly designed benefits can create a "poverty trap." LINK — redistribution is effective at reducing the symptoms of inequality but works best alongside policies addressing its underlying causes (e.g. education, training). This is the exact structure required in workbook Q6.
Common Misconceptions
"Absolute poverty and relative poverty are basically the same thing." Fix: use the Poverty tabs — absolute poverty is about not meeting basic survival needs anywhere in the world; relative poverty is about having much less than others in your own country, and can exist even where absolute poverty does not.
"A higher Gini coefficient means a richer country." Fix: Gini measures the SPREAD of income, not its LEVEL — a country can be very rich on average and still have a high Gini coefficient if that income is very unevenly shared.
"An ageing population is only a problem for poorer countries." Fix: use the population pyramid diagram — ageing is most advanced in some of the world's richest economies (e.g. Japan, South Korea), precisely because falling birth rates and rising life expectancy tend to accompany higher income and better healthcare.

Exam-Precise Glossary
Dependency ratioThe number of dependents (under 15 and over 64) per 100 people of working age.
Lorenz curveA graph plotting the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of population, used to show income inequality.
Gini coefficientA single number (0 to 1) measuring income inequality from a Lorenz curve — 0 is perfect equality, 1 is perfect inequality.
Absolute povertyNot having enough income to meet basic needs (food, water, shelter), regardless of country.
Relative povertyHaving significantly less income than the average person in one's own country.
Optimum population (IB)The population size that maximises output per person for a given quantity of resources and technology.
