Detailed Teaching Notes
Connect the whole course, don't re-teach it
  • Hook: "you've explored eighteen lessons... can you connect them into ONE story, and lead a country at a World Summit?" — resist answering it yourself; this lesson works best when students do the connecting.
  • Story Map: an eight-chapter recap spanning ALL three modules — Scarcity/Markets, Elasticity/Firms/Failure, Money/Measuring, Growth/Jobs/Policy, Trade/Protectionism, Exchange Rates/BoP, Inequality/Poverty/Population, Globalisation/Development — each a tap-to-reveal card. Use as a cold-call springboard: reveal a chapter, then ask a student to add one example from their own life.
  • Concept Connection matching: six real-feeling mini-scenes, each mapping to a concept from a DIFFERENT module. Deliberately scenario-first — students reason from the situation to the vocabulary, the harder and more valuable direction.
  • Misconception Clinic: targets five traps spanning the whole course — trade self-sufficiency, "equal-looking" splits, GDP-per-person vs. genuine wellbeing, MNCs as simply good/bad, and interest rates vs. inflation. Do not rush — these are the most common sources of muddled thinking heading into Tier 2.
  • Key Concept Sort: file ten familiar terms under Change, Well-Being, Fairness, or Intervention. Some placements are debatable by design (e.g. poverty touches both Well-Being and Fairness) — use disagreement as a discussion trigger.
  • The World Summit: the capstone simulation — five summit rounds, each offering two choices that move THREE separate meters (Prosperity, Equity, Sustainability), not just one. This deliberately prevents a single "correct" strategy: every round maps back to a genuine trade-off students met somewhere in the course (trade fairness from L1-2, growth vs. environment from L5, protecting local industry from L2). Debrief by asking whether it's possible to raise all three meters every round — it isn't, by design.
  • Fact-or-opinion game: a whole-course review of the positive/normative distinction threaded through all three modules — read each statement aloud and let students commit to FACT or OPINION before revealing the answer.

Worked Example
Story: "Two countries decide to specialise in what they each do best, then trade — both end up with more than if they'd tried to make everything themselves. As they trade more, a giant company opens a factory in the smaller country, bringing jobs but also competition for local shops. Meanwhile, back home, a government spends more than it collects in tax to fund new schools — a deficit that helps people learn skills for better jobs. But not everyone benefits equally: some families still earn much less than others, even though the economy as a whole is growing. And as factories run faster to keep up with demand, pollution starts to rise, reminding everyone that growth and protecting the planet don't always pull in the same direction." Walk through how EVERY sentence uses a concept from a different module, then ask students to spot which one is which.
Common Misconceptions
"A country should never import anything it could make itself." Fix: use the Misconception Clinic — comparative advantage shows that specialising and trading, even for things you COULD make, can leave everyone better off.
"A perfectly equal-looking split is always fair to everyone." Fix: revisit the Candy Economy idea from earlier in the module — even a "shared" amount can end up very unequally distributed.
"Winning every round of The World Summit has no downside." Fix: ask what each "winning" choice gave up elsewhere — every round is designed around a genuine three-way trade-off, not a free win on all fronts.

Kid-Friendly Glossary — Whole-Course Recap
Opportunity costWhat you give up to get something else — the foundation of every economic choice.
Comparative advantageSpecialising in what you produce relatively best, then trading for the rest.
GDP per personA country's total income divided by its population.
Income inequalityHow unevenly income is shared across a population.
Multinational company (MNC)A company that operates and invests in more than one country.
SustainabilityMeeting today's needs without making it harder for future generations to meet theirs.
