Detailed Teaching Notes
From recall to judgement — with evidence
  • Hook: two students who know exactly the same theory can still earn very different marks — the gap is a real-world example used as genuine EVIDENCE, not decoration. State this explicitly: no new economics today.
  • Command-term drill: separates justify (pick one option, give reasons it beats an alternative), recommend (frame the answer as advice to a decision-maker), and evaluate (the full two-sided, judgement-bearing structure) — the three command terms this course has built toward since M3L1.
  • MCQ warm-up: deliberately drawn from across ALL THREE modules — treat wrong answers as a diagnostic for which earlier lesson needs a 2-minute verbal recap.
  • Short-answer mark-scheme literacy: uses a current-account calculation to show marks are awarded for the METHOD, the correct TOTAL, and the correct deficit/surplus LABEL as three separate credit-bearing steps.
  • Data-response practice: rehearses the (a)/(b)/(c) escalating-depth structure common to both boards, using an exchange-rate depreciation scenario — model reading the WHOLE question stem before writing any answer.
  • Levels of response: put a genuine Band 1 and Band 3 answer to the SAME extended question side by side and have students name the differences themselves before revealing the mark-scheme language (structure / content / judgement).
  • Real-world example bank: the DISTINCTIVE new skill this lesson — drill the NAME → STATE → LINK sequence across four categories (trade blocs, MNCs, currency events, development institutions). Stress that naming an example with NO link back to the argument earns nothing; this is the single most common way strong students lose AO3 marks.
  • IB Part (b) essay structure: walks Claim → Analysis (with a real-world example) → Evaluation → Judgement using the free-trade-and-development example from M3L5. The EVIDENCE inside Analysis is the step students skip most under time pressure.
  • Misconception clinic: re-framed around "why this specific wording costs marks" — e.g. naming an example without linking it, writing one-sided "discuss" answers, or conflating a current account deficit with national debt.
  • Timing & strategy: roughly one minute per mark as a pacing check; a 30–60 second plan before any extended response; know the actual shape of your paper (IGCSE P1/P2 Section A/Section B; IB P1/P2/P3) in advance.
  • Whole-course concept connections recap: re-walks Module 1, Module 2, and Module 3 as three connected movements in one course-long story.
  • Positive/normative game: a whole-course review of the positive/normative distinction threaded through all three modules.

Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "Evaluate whether a developing country should prioritise export-led growth or protecting domestic industries to promote economic development." Model: CLAIM — export-led growth is generally more effective long-term, but limited, temporary protectionism can be justified in specific cases. ANALYSIS + EVIDENCE — specialising via comparative advantage and trading raises a country's total output and can fund development, supported by real-world evidence such as the European Union showing how deeper trade integration can accompany rising living standards among member states. COUNTER — the infant industry argument holds that brand-new domestic industries may need temporary tariff protection to grow strong enough to compete internationally. JUDGEMENT — export-led growth is the stronger long-run strategy for most developing countries, but short-term, clearly time-limited protection for a small number of genuinely infant industries can be a reasonable complement, not a replacement, for openness. This is the exact structure required in workbook Q10.
Common Misconceptions
"Naming a real country or company is itself worth marks." Fix: a real-world example only earns credit when explicitly LINKED back to the argument — naming alone earns nothing.
"Discuss and evaluate mean the same thing — just write everything I know." Fix: discuss only requires presenting more than one side; evaluate additionally requires weighing those sides to reach a supported judgement.
"A current account deficit and national debt are the same thing" (written in an exam answer). Fix: a current account deficit is a FLOW (one year's imbalance); national debt is a STOCK (the total accumulated amount owed, built up over many years).
Teaching Resources
Exam-Precise Glossary
Command termThe instruction word in a question (e.g. justify, recommend, evaluate) that fixes exactly what kind of response is required and how much depth earns credit.
Real-world evidence (RWE)A named real example (country, company, institution, or event) used to support an argument — must be explicitly linked back to the point being made to earn credit.
Levels of responseAn IGCSE mark-scheme format that grades an extended answer holistically into bands based on structure, content, and judgement, rather than awarding isolated single marks.
Claim → Analysis → Evaluation → JudgementThe scaffold for a full IB Part (b) essay: a clear position, the reasoning/mechanism with evidence, a genuine opposing consideration, and a final supported (usually conditional) conclusion.
Justify / RecommendCommand terms requiring a clear position AND explicit reasons why it beats at least one alternative — often framed as advice to a named decision-maker.
Current account deficit vs. national debtA current account deficit is one year's imbalance in trade and income flows (a flow); national debt is the total accumulated amount a government owes (a stock).
