Extended Response · mock exam · Evaluate
10. Evaluate whether a developing country should prioritise export-led growth (free trade) or protecting domestic industries (protectionism) to promote economic development. Structure your answer as CLAIM → ANALYSIS → COUNTER → JUDGEMENT, referring to at least THREE concepts from this course (e.g. comparative advantage, tariffs, the infant industry argument, the current account) AND at least ONE named real-world example or institution as evidence. [8]
Answer Key & Mark Scheme Notes
1. B — Comparative advantage. [1 mark]
2. B — A tariff. [1 mark]
3. B — Appreciate. [1 mark]
4. Trade in goods balance: the difference between the value of physical goods a country exports and imports. Current account: a BROADER measure combining trade in goods, trade in services, primary income, and secondary income. The terms are kept separate because the current account can be in surplus or deficit even when the trade-in-goods balance alone points the other way — services, income, and remittances can shift the overall picture. [1 mark each definition, 1 mark for the distinction]
5. It is imprecise because MNCs create both benefits (jobs, tax revenue, technology transfer) AND costs (competition for local firms, profits flowing back to the home country, environmental pressure) — "always good" ignores the trade-off. Correct version: "Multinational companies can bring real benefits to a host country, such as jobs and tax revenue, but also real costs, such as competition for local businesses." [1 mark for identifying the overgeneralisation, 1 mark for a correctly worded rewrite naming at least one benefit and one cost]
6. (a) % change in exchange rate = (1,400 − 1,200) ÷ 1,200 × 100 = +16.7% (the currency needs MORE units per $1, i.e. depreciation). (b) % change in export revenue = (58 − 50) ÷ 50 × 100 = +16%. [2–3 marks per correct calculation with method shown]
7. The depreciation made Verdania's exports cheaper in foreign-currency terms, boosting demand and raising export revenue from $50bn to $58bn. However, a weaker currency also makes IMPORTS more expensive in domestic-currency terms, which can raise the cost of imported goods for domestic consumers and contribute to inflationary pressure — a common downside of depreciation. [2 marks correctly explaining the export effect from the data, 2 marks correctly reasoning the import/consumer downside]
8. Government reason (accept any reasonable, e.g.): higher export revenue can boost domestic output and jobs in export industries, supporting growth. Critic's objection (accept any reasonable, e.g.): more expensive imports can raise the cost of living for consumers and raise costs for firms that rely on imported inputs, potentially fuelling inflation. [2 marks per reason: 1 for a valid reason, 1 for linking it explicitly to the data/scenario]
9. Accept any THREE of: (1) define free trade and comparative advantage precisely rather than assuming the reader knows; (2) add ANALYSIS — explain the mechanism (specialising in comparative advantage and trading raises total output for both trading partners); (3) add a COUNTER — countries with weak infrastructure, institutions, or negotiating power may capture a much smaller share of the gains, so benefits are NOT necessarily equal; (4) end with a JUDGEMENT that is CONDITIONAL rather than absolute (e.g. "free trade tends to raise total output, but how EQUALLY the gains are shared depends heavily on each country's starting infrastructure and institutions"). [1 mark per valid, specific improvement named — must describe what Band 1 is missing, not just restate the answer]
10. Band 3 (6–8 marks): CLAIM (e.g. "export-led growth is generally more effective long-term, but limited, temporary protectionism can be justified in specific cases"); ANALYSIS (specialising via comparative advantage and trading raises a country's total output and can fund development — supported by real-world evidence such as the European Union showing how deeper trade integration can accompany rising living standards among member states); COUNTER (the infant industry argument holds that brand-new domestic industries may need temporary tariff protection to grow strong enough to compete internationally, since immediate full exposure to established foreign competitors could prevent them from ever developing); JUDGEMENT (a supported conclusion such as "export-led growth is the stronger long-run strategy for most developing countries, but short-term, clearly time-limited protection for a small number of genuinely infant industries can be a reasonable complement, not a replacement, for openness"). Band 2 (3–5): names relevant concepts (comparative advantage, tariffs) but the four-part structure is incomplete — e.g. no real counter-argument, no real-world example, or a judgement that is just a restated claim. Band 1 (1–2): a one-sided assertion ("free trade always works best" or "protectionism always works best") with no named concept, no real-world evidence, and no conditional judgement.
