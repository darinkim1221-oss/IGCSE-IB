Detailed Teaching Notes
From recall to judgement
  • Hook: two students who know exactly the same facts can still earn very different marks — the gap is HOW they write, not WHAT they know. State this explicitly: no new economics today.
  • Command-term drill: separates discuss (present more than one side), evaluate (present more than one side AND weigh them to reach a judgement), and to what extent (state a degree/condition, not a flat yes/no) — the three most conflated command terms across both boards.
  • MCQ warm-up: deliberately drawn from across the whole module — treat wrong answers as a diagnostic for which earlier lesson needs a 2-minute verbal recap.
  • Short-answer mark-scheme literacy: uses an unemployment-rate calculation to show marks are awarded for the FORMULA, the SUBSTITUTION, and the labelled FINAL ANSWER as three separate credit-bearing steps.
  • Data-response practice: rehearses the (a)/(b)/(c) escalating-depth structure common to both boards — model reading the WHOLE question stem before writing any answer.
  • Levels of response: put a genuine Band 1 and Band 3 answer to the SAME 8-mark question side by side and have students name the differences themselves before revealing the mark-scheme language (structure / content / judgement).
  • Evaluation paragraph: walks CLAIM → ANALYSIS → COUNTER → JUDGEMENT using an interest-rate example from M2L5. The COUNTER is the step students skip most under time pressure — without it, the JUDGEMENT collapses into a restated claim.
  • IB "discuss" structure: a first rehearsal of the mini Part (b) format — two genuinely distinct sides, then a supported judgement in one or two sentences, NOT a full essay.
  • Misconception clinic: re-framed around "why this specific wording costs marks" — e.g. conflating deficit with debt, or GDP-per-person with genuine wellbeing, signal a shaky grasp of the underlying model, not just sloppy wording.
  • Timing & strategy: roughly one minute per mark as a pacing check; a 30–60 second plan before any extended response; let the command word set the depth.
  • Concept connections recap: re-walks L1–L2, L4, and L5 as three connected movements in one macro story.
  • Positive/normative game: a module-wide review of the positive/normative distinction threaded through the whole module, applied to statements spanning fiscal, monetary, and supply-side policy.

Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "Evaluate whether monetary policy or fiscal policy is more effective at controlling inflation." Model: CLAIM — monetary policy is generally faster-acting, but fiscal policy can be more targeted. ANALYSIS — a central bank can change interest rates quickly, and higher rates directly cool demand-pull inflation by raising borrowing costs (link to interest rates/macro aims, M2L5). COUNTER — fiscal policy (e.g. cutting government spending) can also reduce demand-pull inflation and may allow more targeted cuts to specific sectors, though it is slower to implement due to budget/political processes and can affect the government's own budget deficit. JUDGEMENT — monetary policy likely controls demand-pull inflation faster in most cases, but fiscal policy may be preferable when a government wants to protect specific groups from side effects such as rising unemployment. This is the exact structure required in workbook Q10.
Common Misconceptions
"Discuss and evaluate mean the same thing — just write everything I know." Fix: discuss only requires presenting more than one side; evaluate additionally requires weighing those sides to reach a supported judgement.
"A budget deficit and national debt are the same thing" (written in an exam answer). Fix: a deficit is a FLOW (one year's spending minus revenue); debt is a STOCK (the total accumulated amount owed, built up over many years).
"Inflation is always bad for everyone." Fix: inflation hurts savers and those on fixed incomes, but can benefit borrowers who repay with money worth less than when they borrowed it — always name the specific group affected.

Exam-Precise Glossary
Command termThe instruction word in a question (e.g. define, explain, discuss, evaluate) that fixes exactly what kind of response is required and how much depth earns credit.
Levels of responseAn IGCSE mark-scheme format that grades an extended answer holistically into bands (e.g. Band 1–3) based on structure, content, and judgement, rather than awarding isolated single marks.
Claim → Analysis → Counter → JudgementThe four-part scaffold for a full evaluation paragraph: a clear position, the reasoning/mechanism behind it, a genuine opposing consideration, and a final supported (usually conditional) conclusion.
"Discuss" (IB)Requires presenting more than one side of an issue; does not by itself require a final judgement (that is "evaluate" or "to what extent").
"To what extent" (IB)Requires a judgement stated as a DEGREE or CONDITION, not a flat yes/no — e.g. "largely… but only when…".
Budget deficit vs. national debtA deficit is one year's spending exceeding revenue (a flow); national debt is the total accumulated amount owed (a stock), built up over many years of deficits.
