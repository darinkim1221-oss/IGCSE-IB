Extended Response · mock exam · Evaluate
10. Evaluate whether monetary policy or fiscal policy is more effective at controlling inflation. Structure your answer as CLAIM → ANALYSIS → COUNTER → JUDGEMENT, referring to at least THREE concepts from this module (e.g. interest rates, government spending, budget deficit, unemployment, macro aims). [8]
Answer Key & Mark Scheme Notes
1. B — Budget deficit. [1 mark]
2. C — Frictional unemployment. [1 mark]
3. B — Monetary policy. [1 mark]
4. Budget deficit: when government spending exceeds tax revenue in ONE year. National debt: the total accumulated amount a government owes, built up over many years of deficits (minus any surpluses). The terms are kept separate because a deficit is a FLOW (one year's gap) while debt is a STOCK (the total accumulated balance) — a country can run a small deficit while still holding large accumulated debt, or vice versa. [1 mark each definition, 1 mark for the flow/stock distinction]
5. It is imprecise because inflation affects different groups differently — it hurts savers and those on fixed incomes, but can actually benefit borrowers who repay with money worth less than when they borrowed it. Correct version: "Inflation tends to hurt savers and those on fixed incomes, but can benefit borrowers." [1 mark for identifying the overgeneralisation, 1 mark for a correctly worded rewrite naming at least one winner and one loser]
6. (a) Inflation rate before = (106 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 6%. Inflation rate after = (103 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 3%. (b) Change in unemployment rate = 5.8% − 4.5% = a rise of 1.3 percentage points. [2 marks per correct inflation-rate calculation with method shown, 1 mark for the correctly calculated percentage-point change]
7. Raising interest rates made borrowing more expensive, so households and firms spent and invested less — reducing pressure on prices and bringing the CPI-implied inflation rate down from 6% to 3%. However, the SAME policy likely contributed to the rise in unemployment from 4.5% to 5.8%, since reduced spending and investment can mean fewer jobs — a classic side effect (trade-off) of using interest rates to fight inflation. [2 marks correctly explaining the inflation effect, 2 marks correctly reasoning the unemployment side effect from the data]
8. Central bank reason (accept any reasonable, e.g.): keeping inflation low protects the value of money and supports the "stable prices" macro aim, even at some short-term cost. Critic's objection (accept any reasonable, e.g.): the rise in unemployment (4.5% → 5.8%) shows real workers lost jobs or opportunities as a direct consequence, conflicting with the "full employment" macro aim. [2 marks per reason: 1 for a valid reason, 1 for linking it explicitly to the data/scenario]
9. Accept any THREE of: (1) define what monetary policy/interest rates are precisely, rather than assuming the reader knows; (2) add ANALYSIS — explain the mechanism (higher rates raise the cost of borrowing, reducing spending and investment, which cools demand-pull inflation); (3) add a COUNTER — interest rate rises can also raise unemployment and may be less effective if inflation is COST-PUSH rather than demand-pull, or if confidence is already low; (4) end with a JUDGEMENT that is CONDITIONAL rather than absolute (e.g. "interest rate rises work best against demand-pull inflation in a healthy economy, but are a blunter tool against cost-push inflation or during a fragile recovery"). [1 mark per valid, specific improvement named — must describe what Band 1 is missing, not just restate the answer]
10. Band 3 (6–8 marks): CLAIM (e.g. "monetary policy is generally faster-acting, but fiscal policy can be more targeted"); ANALYSIS (a central bank can change interest rates quickly, and higher rates directly cool demand-pull inflation by raising borrowing costs — link to interest rates/macro aims); COUNTER (fiscal policy, e.g. cutting government spending, can also reduce demand-pull inflation and may allow more targeted cuts to specific sectors, though it is slower to implement due to budget/political processes and can affect the government's own budget deficit); JUDGEMENT (a supported conclusion such as "monetary policy likely controls demand-pull inflation faster in most cases, but fiscal policy may be preferable when a government wants to protect specific groups from the side effects, such as rising unemployment"). Band 2 (3–5): names relevant concepts (interest rates, government spending) but the four-part structure is incomplete — e.g. no real counter-argument, or a judgement that is just a restated claim. Band 1 (1–2): a one-sided assertion ("raising interest rates always works best") with no named economic concept, no counter, and no conditional judgement.
