Detailed Teaching Notes
Connect it, don't re-teach it
  • Hook: "you've explored five big ideas this module... can you connect them into ONE story?" — resist answering it yourself; this lesson works best when students do the connecting.
  • Story Map: a five-chapter recap — Money & Banking, Measuring the Economy, Growth/Jobs/Prices, Policy Toolkit, Macro Aims & Conflicts — each a tap-to-reveal card. Use as a cold-call springboard: reveal a chapter, then ask a student to add one example from their own life.
  • Concept Connection matching: five real-feeling mini-scenes, each mapping to a concept from a DIFFERENT lesson. Deliberately scenario-first — students reason from the situation to the vocabulary, the harder and more valuable direction.
  • Misconception Clinic: targets three traps — money vs. wealth, deficit vs. bankruptcy, and GDP-per-person vs. genuine wellbeing. Do not rush — these are the most common source of muddled thinking at Tier 2.
  • Key Concept Sort: file eight familiar terms under Change, Well-Being, or Intervention. Some placements are debatable by design (e.g. unemployment touches both Change and Well-Being) — use disagreement as a discussion trigger.
  • Save the Economy: the capstone simulation — five news bulletins, each offering two policy choices that move a shared "stability" meter. Every round maps directly back to a lesson: L5's fiscal/monetary tools (Bulletins 1–2), L5's supply-side measures (Bulletin 3), L5's budget trade-off (Bulletin 4), and L5's growth-vs-environment conflict (Bulletin 5). Debrief by asking which choice surprised them most.
  • Fact-or-opinion game: a module-wide review of the positive/normative distinction threaded through the whole module — read each statement aloud and let students commit to FACT or OPINION before revealing the answer.

Worked Example
Story: "A country's economy slows down, so the government spends more on roads and schools — fiscal policy in action. This creates jobs, but soon prices start rising a little too fast, so the central bank raises interest rates a touch — monetary policy stepping in. Some workers whose factory jobs disappeared still can't find new roles, so the government funds retraining — a supply-side measure. All this spending has left a budget deficit, so the government must decide whether to keep investing or cut back. And just as things stabilise, fast-growing factories start polluting more, reminding everyone that growth and environmental sustainability don't always pull in the same direction." Walk through how EVERY sentence uses a concept from a different lesson, then ask students to spot which one is which.
Common Misconceptions
"Having more money in your wallet always means you're wealthier." Fix: use the Misconception Clinic — if prices have risen a lot (inflation), the same amount of money buys less than before.
"A budget deficit means the country is bankrupt." Fix: a deficit means spending was higher than tax revenue in ONE year — it isn't the same as being unable to pay at all.
"Winning every round of Save the Economy has no downside." Fix: ask what each "winning" choice gave up elsewhere — every round in the game is designed around a genuine trade-off, not a free win.

Kid-Friendly Glossary — Module Recap
GDPThe total value of everything a country produces in a year.
RecessionA period where total spending and production shrink instead of growing.
UnemploymentWhen someone who wants a job and can work cannot find one.
InflationWhen the average price of things rises over time, so money buys less.
Fiscal policyUsing government spending and taxation to influence the economy.
Monetary policyUsing tools like interest rates to influence the economy.
