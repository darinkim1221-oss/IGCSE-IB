Key Concepts & the IB Lens
Intervention (primary)Efficiency (primary)Equity (primary)
M1L4 is the course's first formal encounter with government intervention diagrams and the first structured introduction to two-sided analytical writing. Where M1L3 built calculation fluency, M1L4 builds diagram fluency under a policy lens — every diagram drawn here must be immediately followed by "who does this help, and who does this hurt?" This is also the drip map's first appearance of stakeholder analysis and the "examine" command term (two-sided, no final judgement), both of which recur and intensify through the rest of the IGCSE/IB analysis arc. Treat the discipline of NOT concluding — holding both sides seriously — as being just as important a deliverable this lesson as the diagrams themselves.
Detailed Teaching Notes
From hypothesis to diagram to stakeholder
Open with the hook framed as a testable hypothesis (slide s0): "every time a government intervenes in a market, someone gains — and someone loses — even when the goal is fairness." This reframes the lesson as building a tool to identify exactly who, not just a vocabulary list. Run the command-term drill (slide s1) on market failure and price ceilings across Define / State / Describe before any diagrams appear.
The price ceiling / price floor diagrams (slide s2) are the first of the four diagrams the wiki flags as drawing-REQUIRED at IGCSE — treat them as load-bearing exam skills, not optional illustration. Drill the sequence every time: (1) draw standard D and S with a labelled equilibrium; (2) draw the ceiling/floor as a horizontal line at the legally fixed price; (3) identify which curve (D or S) is now the binding constraint at that price; (4) read off and shade the resulting gap; (5) name it correctly — shortage for a ceiling (Qd > Qs), surplus for a floor (Qs > Qd). A common Cambridge mark-loss is students drawing the ceiling ABOVE or the floor BELOW equilibrium — physically check direction with students before they practise independently.
The indirect tax / subsidy diagrams (slide s3) are the second required pair. The core visual insight to land: a tax makes selling more costly at every quantity, so the ENTIRE supply curve shifts up/left (not just one point moves); a subsidy does the reverse. New equilibrium price and quantity change together — students should be able to state the direction of both without re-deriving from scratch (tax → price up, quantity down; subsidy → price down, quantity up). The tax wedge / subsidy-per-unit is the vertical distance, at the NEW quantity, between the new supply curve and the OLD supply curve — this is the step students most often skip; insist it is drawn and labelled every time.
The wider toolkit (slide s4) — regulation/ban, direct provision, quotas, nationalisation/privatisation — deliberately does NOT require a D/S diagram; make this explicit so students don't try to force every tool onto a price-based graph. These tools work by changing what is legal, who owns/produces something, or how much may be produced/consumed, rather than by shifting a curve.
The stakeholder analysis table (slide s5, rent control) is this lesson's core AO2 structured-analysis skill. Model the three-column habit explicitly: Stakeholder → Winner/Loser/Mixed → Reason. Push students to go beyond "landlords lose money" toward the full chain: rent ceiling → landlords receive less revenue per unit → some withdraw flats from the rental market → fewer flats available → shortage worsens for renters still searching. This is the "2+ link cause-effect chain" the wiki drip map names explicitly for this lesson.
The externality & common pool resource classification (slide s6) is explicitly flagged as IB enrichment — the four-way grid (negative/positive × production/consumption) and the CPR/tragedy-of-commons framework do not appear in IGCSE 0455. Students should still recognise real-world examples of each from Tier 1's factory-by-the-river and fishing-lake material; this slide adds the formal classification vocabulary on top of intuition already built.
The merit/demerit/public goods/monopoly cases tour (slide s7) connects the toolkit to real policy: merit goods are under-consumed and often subsidised/directly provided (education, vaccination); demerit goods are over-consumed and often taxed/regulated (tobacco, sugary drinks); public goods are under-provided by the market due to the free-rider problem and are usually directly provided (lighthouses, national defence); monopoly abuse of market power is a distinct failure mode addressed through competition regulation rather than a price/quantity diagram.
The "discuss" paragraph (slide s8, plastic bag ban) is the course's first formal two-sided writing exercise. The critical teaching move: stop students the moment they reach for a verdict ("so overall, I think...") — that move belongs to evaluate, taught later. At this stage, "examine" success means both sides are equally developed, each uses a named economic concept, and the paragraph ends without picking a winner.
Visuals & Diagrams
📊
The four required intervention diagrams: price ceiling (horizontal line below equilibrium → shortage), price floor (horizontal line above equilibrium → surplus), indirect tax (supply shifts up/left → higher price, lower quantity, tax wedge), subsidy (supply shifts down/right → lower price, higher quantity, subsidy wedge). All four use the same base D/S diagram — teach them as one skeleton with four different applications, not four unrelated diagrams.
Important — do NOT draw an externality diagram at this tier: the wiki's syllabus verification note is explicit — "the 2027–2029 syllabus states explicitly that demand & supply diagrams relating to market failure are NOT required at IGCSE. Externality/welfare-loss diagrams are IB-level." The four diagrams above (ceiling, floor, tax, subsidy) ARE required at IGCSE and should be drilled heavily. A formal marginal-social-cost/benefit externality diagram with a welfare-loss triangle should only be introduced with IB-track classes, and flagged clearly as beyond IGCSE 0455 if any IGCSE-track student encounters it via wider reading.
Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "A government introduces a minimum wage above the free-market equilibrium wage for unskilled labour. Draw a diagram to show the effect, and analyse the impact on workers and employers." Model: (1) draw standard labour D/S with equilibrium wage; (2) draw the minimum wage as a horizontal line above equilibrium; (3) identify Qs > Qd at that wage — a surplus of labour, i.e. unemployment; (4) stakeholder chain — workers who keep jobs are better off (higher wage), but firms now demand less labour at the higher wage, so some workers who would have been hired at the old equilibrium are not. This is the exact structure used in workbook Q4 and Q8 — walk the diagram AND the stakeholder chain together on the board before students attempt them independently.
Common Misconceptions
"A price ceiling/floor moves the equilibrium point." Fix: it does not — the equilibrium stays where D and S cross. The ceiling/floor is a separate, legally fixed horizontal line that FORCES the market away from that equilibrium, creating a shortage or surplus at the fixed price instead.
"A tax/subsidy shifts demand, not supply." Fix: taxes and subsidies in this syllabus are modelled as changes to the SELLER's cost of supplying the good, so it is the supply curve that shifts — demand is unaffected because buyers' willingness to pay at each quantity hasn't changed.
"Intervention always helps consumers and hurts producers (or vice versa)." Fix: every intervention has a genuine stakeholder MIX — a price ceiling helps tenants who keep their flat but hurts renters who can no longer find one at all. Insist students name at least one winner AND one loser inside the same stakeholder group before accepting an analysis as complete.
Exam Adapter Slot
Source: Wiki §7 drip map, row M1 L4 ("Analysis")
Skill focus this lesson: stakeholder analysis table; 2+ link cause-effect chains; first intervention diagrams. Command terms in play: analyse, distinguish, apply.
IGCSE (AO2)
Structured analysis paragraphs; first Section B short-answer practice (4–6 marks). Reward a clear cause-effect-consequence chain over a list of disconnected facts — e.g. "tax raises cost of production → supply shifts left → price rises → quantity falls → consumers pay more, producers sell less."
Practice move: give students a bare policy ("a subsidy on solar panels") and have them build the full chain from mechanism to at least one named stakeholder consequence, in one continuous sentence.
IB (AO2)
Cause-effect-consequence chains extended into an introduction to "examine" — two-sided, with NO final judgement yet. That final "which side wins" move is "evaluate," introduced later in the course.
Practice move: for any intervention studied this lesson, require one FOR chain and one AGAINST chain of equal length before moving on — exactly the discipline modelled in the plastic-bag "discuss" slide.
Extension Activities
  • Real intervention hunt: find a real news story about a government intervention (a new tax, subsidy, price cap, or ban) and identify which of the four diagrams or wider-toolkit tools applies, then build a 3-stakeholder table for it.
  • Black market mini-case: [low-priority enrichment] research a real historical price ceiling (e.g. rent control, wartime price controls) and discuss what happened in the informal/black market as a consequence.
  • [IB] Externality classification challenge: collect five real-world examples (news, local community) and classify each into the four-way negative/positive × production/consumption grid.

    Exam-Precise Glossary
    Market failureWhen the free market fails to allocate resources efficiently — the price mechanism does not produce the socially optimal outcome.
    ExternalityA spillover cost or benefit of a transaction falling on a third party not reflected in the market price.
    Price ceilingA legal MAXIMUM price set below equilibrium — causes a shortage (Qd > Qs).
    Price floorA legal MINIMUM price set above equilibrium — causes a surplus (Qs > Qd).
    Indirect taxA tax on spending/production that shifts supply up/left, raising price and lowering quantity traded.
    SubsidyA payment to producers that shifts supply down/right, lowering price and raising quantity traded.
    Stakeholder analysisIdentifying who wins, who loses, and why, for a given market outcome or intervention.
    Common pool resource[IB enrichment] Non-excludable but rival — overuse by individuals can collapse the shared resource (tragedy of the commons).
    Public goodNon-excludable AND non-rival — under-provided by the free market due to the free-rider problem; typically directly provided by government.
    Key Takeaways
    1. Four diagrams are drawing-REQUIRED at IGCSE — price ceiling, price floor, indirect tax, subsidy — drill the shared D/S skeleton, not four separate diagrams. 2. Externality/welfare-loss diagrams are IB-level enrichment only — never required or tested at IGCSE 0455. 3. Every intervention has a genuine stakeholder mix; insist on a named winner AND a named loser before accepting an analysis as complete. 4. "Examine"/"discuss" at this stage means two sides held with equal seriousness and NO final verdict — that move is "evaluate," introduced later. 5. The wider toolkit (regulation, direct provision, quotas, nationalisation) works without a price diagram — don't force every tool onto a D/S graph.
