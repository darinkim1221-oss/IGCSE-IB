Extended Response · exam-style
11. Discuss whether a government should tax sugary drinks to reduce consumption. Consider both the case FOR the tax and the case AGAINST it, referring to at least one relevant economic concept from this lesson (e.g. externality, demerit good, price elasticity, stakeholder impact). Do not reach a final judgement — this is an "examine," not an "evaluate." [8]
Answer Key & Mark Scheme Notes
1. Market failure occurs when the free market, left to itself, fails to allocate scarce resources efficiently — the outcome the price mechanism produces is not the socially optimal one. [1 mark for "free market fails to allocate efficiently," 1 mark for a correct cause or consequence, e.g. externalities, public goods, or common pool resource overuse]
2. An externality is a spillover cost or benefit of a transaction that falls on a third party who did not agree to it and is not reflected in the market price. A negative externality imposes a cost on third parties (e.g. a factory's noise disturbing a nearby school). A positive externality creates a benefit for third parties (e.g. a well-maintained private garden that beautifies the whole street for passers-by). [1 mark definition, 1 mark correct negative distinction, 1 mark correct positive distinction, 1 mark for two valid original examples]
3. Standard D/S diagram: downward-sloping D, upward-sloping S, equilibrium (Pe, Qe) marked at their intersection. A horizontal ceiling line drawn BELOW Pe. At the ceiling price, Qd (read off the demand curve) is greater than Qs (read off the supply curve) — the horizontal gap between these two points, at the ceiling price, is the shortage, and should be marked/shaded. [1 mark correctly shaped D and S with labelled equilibrium, 1 mark ceiling correctly drawn below equilibrium, 1 mark Qd/Qs points at the ceiling correctly identified, 1 mark shortage clearly labelled]
4. Standard D/S diagram for labour (wage on the price axis). A horizontal floor line drawn ABOVE the equilibrium wage. At the floor wage, Qs (labour supplied) is greater than Qd (labour demanded) — this gap is the surplus of labour, i.e. unemployment, and should be marked/shaded. [1 mark correctly shaped D and S with labelled equilibrium, 1 mark floor correctly drawn above equilibrium, 1 mark Qs/Qd points at the floor correctly identified, 1 mark surplus/unemployment clearly labelled]
5. Original S curve drawn, then a second S curve shifted up and to the left (parallel or near-parallel), representing the higher cost of supplying each unit once the tax is added. New equilibrium at a higher price and lower quantity than before. A vertical line/bracket at the new quantity connecting the new (higher) price consumers pay to the (lower) price producers receive on the original supply curve — this vertical distance is the tax wedge, and should be labelled. [1 mark original S and D, 1 mark new S shifted correctly up/left, 1 mark new equilibrium at higher P / lower Q, 1 mark tax wedge correctly identified, 1 mark full correct labelling]
6. Original S curve drawn, then a second S curve shifted down and to the right, representing the lower cost of supplying each unit once the subsidy is applied. New equilibrium at a lower price and higher quantity than before. A vertical line/bracket at the new quantity connecting the new (lower) price consumers pay to the (higher) price producers receive on the original supply curve — this vertical distance is the subsidy per unit, and should be labelled. [1 mark original S and D, 1 mark new S shifted correctly down/right, 1 mark new equilibrium at lower P / higher Q, 1 mark subsidy per unit correctly identified, 1 mark full correct labelling]
7. Regulation/ban → D (bans the sale of single-use plastic straws). Direct provision → C (builds and runs free public libraries). Quota → B (limits fishing licences issued each year). Nationalisation → A (state ownership of the water company). [1 mark per correct match]
8. Workers who keep their jobs: Winner — they now earn a higher wage than the free-market equilibrium would have paid them. Workers who lose their jobs / can't find one: Loser — at the higher minimum wage, quantity of labour demanded by firms falls, so some workers who would have been hired at the lower equilibrium wage are not. Employers: Loser (or Mixed) — labour costs rise per worker employed, squeezing profit margins or forcing a reduction in hours/staff, though some may pass costs to consumers via higher prices. [2 marks per row: 1 for correct classification, 1 for a valid supporting reason]
9. Steel mill smoke damaging crops → Negative production externality (harm arises from the act of PRODUCING). Loud party disturbing neighbours → Negative consumption externality (harm arises from the act of CONSUMING/enjoying the party). Orchard blossoms supporting a beekeeper → Positive production externality (benefit arises from the orchard's production activity). Tidy garden raising property values → Positive consumption externality (benefit arises from the homeowner's private consumption/enjoyment choice, gardening). [1 mark per correct classification]
10. A common pool resource is non-excludable (no single boat can stop others fishing) but rival (fish caught by one boat are not available to any other boat). Because no individual boat owns the fish, each boat has an incentive to catch as much as possible before someone else does — there is no individual reward for restraint, since fish left uncaught will likely just be taken by a rival boat instead. If every boat reasons this way simultaneously, the total catch can exceed the rate at which the fish stock regenerates, causing the stock to collapse — even though every boat, taken individually, would have preferred a sustainable outcome. [1 mark correct non-excludable+rival definition, 1 mark individual-incentive reasoning ("no reward for restraint"), 2 marks for correctly explaining how individually rational choices aggregate into a collectively irrational/destructive outcome]
11. Band 3 (6–8 marks): develops both sides with equal seriousness. FOR: sugary drinks are a demerit good with a negative consumption externality (higher public healthcare costs from diet-related illness borne by taxpayers generally, not just the drinker); a tax raises price, and if demand is even moderately elastic, reduces quantity consumed while raising government revenue that can fund health programmes. AGAINST: demand for sugary drinks may be relatively inelastic (habitual/addictive consumption), so a tax mainly raises revenue without much changing behaviour, and is regressive — it takes a larger share of income from lower-income consumers who spend a higher proportion of their budget on such goods. A strong answer explicitly names a concept (externality, demerit good, PED, regressive taxation) on each side and reaches no final verdict. Band 2 (3–5): one side well developed, other side thin or purely assertive; some correct concept use. Band 1 (1–2): one-sided argument, no named economic concept, or a premature final judgement without acknowledging the other side.
