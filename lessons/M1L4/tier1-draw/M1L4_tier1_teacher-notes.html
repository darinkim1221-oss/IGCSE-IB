Learning Objectives
By the end, students can…
explain, in plain language, why a decision made by one person or company can create a real cost or benefit for someone else who never agreed to it
sort real examples into "good spillover" and "bad spillover" and justify why
describe why a shared, unowned resource (like a fishing lake) can be overused and even collapse, even when nobody individually wants that outcome
explain the free-rider problem using a concrete example (a streetlight or a public fireworks show)
name at least two tools a government could use to fix a spillover or protect a shared resource (tax, subsidy, ban, direct provision)
construct one argument for and one argument against a real policy question (taxing candy like cigarettes)

This lesson is the first time students meet the idea that markets can go WRONG — that the price a buyer and seller agree on doesn't always capture the full story. Every activity in this lesson is designed to let students feel a spillover or a shared-resource problem before any formal vocabulary (externality, public good, tragedy of the commons) is attached to it. That sequencing matters: students who feel "wait, that's not fair — the factory didn't pay for the mess" have a much stronger anchor for the formal externality diagrams they'll meet in Tier 2 than students who are handed the definition cold.
Detailed Teaching Notes
Feel the unfairness before you name it
Open with the factory-vs-village hook and let students react before revealing anything formal — most students will immediately sense "that's not fair" without needing the word "externality." Protect that instinct; it's the seed of the whole lesson.
The factory demo (slide s1) is the core teaching device: clicking "Produce More" grows the factory's profit while visibly draining the river's health and fish count. Make the point explicit and repeat it: the factory's own books never show a cost for the river — the cost has "spilled" onto people outside the transaction. This is externality intuition without ever using the word "externality" until after the pattern is felt.
The spillover spectrum sort (slide s2) deliberately covers both directions — positive AND negative spillovers — since students often assume "spillover" always means something bad. Beekeeping and vaccination are useful counter-examples that a spillover can be a gift, not just a cost.
The free-rider streetlight experiment (slide s3) introduces public goods through a concrete, countable example: if too few neighbors pay, the light never gets built; but once it IS built, everyone gets it — including the neighbor who never paid. Let students sit with the unfairness of that before naming it "the free-rider problem."
Run the standalone Fishing Lake game (companion file) as either an in-class centerpiece or the homework kickoff — it is this lesson's version of the classic "shared bowl of goldfish crackers" tragedy-of-the-commons activity, translated into a scored, replayable browser format. The key discovery it's built to produce: fishing HIGH every round feels individually smart but can collapse the shared lake by round 3; the "winning" strategy usually requires easing off as the shared stock visibly thins — a direct, felt lesson in why shared resources need a rule, quota, or shared restraint that individual incentive alone won't produce.
The candy-tax slider and debate (slide s4) is the lesson's bridge to real policy: as the slider raises the tax, quantity bought falls and tax revenue changes — then students must pick a side and receive the counter-argument, modelling that real policy questions have genuine trade-offs, not a single "correct" answer at this level.
Close with the government toolbox sort (slide s5 — tax, subsidy, ban/regulation, direct provision) and the rapid round (slide s6, "market failure or working fine?") as consolidation — the rapid round is a strong cold-call tool since answers are binary and quick to justify aloud.
Visuals & Diagrams
🌊
The river-health meter: a simple bar that drains as the factory produces more — purely a visual metaphor for "a cost that isn't showing up on anyone's bill." No demand/supply diagram is used or required at this tier — the wiki's syllabus note explicitly flags that formal externality and welfare-loss diagrams are IB-enrichment only and NOT required at IGCSE, so Tier 1 should never attempt to pre-teach that diagram either.
Tip: if a student says "the factory should just be allowed to do whatever makes the most profit," redirect to the river-health meter and ask "whose money paid for that damage?" — let them discover that "nobody's, yet" is exactly the problem a government intervention is meant to fix.
Worked Example
Story: "A small town shares one fishing lake. There are no rules about how many fish each boat can catch. Every boat owner reasons the same way: 'if I don't catch this fish now, another boat will.' By the third summer, the lake has almost no fish left, and every boat — even the most careful one — earns far less than in year one." Walk through why: no single boat "decided" to destroy the lake, but each boat's individually reasonable choice added up to a collective disaster — this is the tragedy of the commons, and it's exactly what the Fishing Lake game is built to let students discover for themselves.
Common Misconceptions
"If something is free to use, everyone will use it responsibly." Fix: point to the Fishing Lake game — nobody owning the resource is exactly why no single boat has a personal incentive to hold back, even when everyone would prefer the lake to survive.
"A spillover is always something bad." Fix: use the beekeeping and vaccination examples from the spectrum sort — a spillover can be a benefit nobody paid for, not just a cost nobody agreed to.
"The free-rider is doing something wrong/illegal." Fix: free-riding isn't cheating in a rule-breaking sense — it's a rational response to a good nobody can be excluded from. The "fix" is a policy tool (tax, direct provision), not blame.
Think Like an Economist
Prompt for open discussion or journaling: "Think of something in your own neighborhood, school, or family that everyone shares but nobody personally owns (a shared fridge, a school printer, a park). What could happen if everyone used it as much as they personally wanted, with no rules at all?"
Extension Activities
  • Spillover hunt: with a family member, find one real example from your own week of a decision that spilled a cost or benefit onto someone else who wasn't part of the decision.
  • Design a rule: invent one simple rule that could have saved the Fishing Lake game's lake from collapsing, and explain why it would work.
  • Policy debate write-up: pick one real "should it be taxed?" question (sugary drinks, plastic bags, candy) and write both a FOR and an AGAINST argument in full sentences.

    Kid-Friendly Glossary
    Spillover (externality)A cost or benefit from a decision that lands on someone who wasn't part of making it.
    Shared resourceSomething everyone can use but nobody personally owns — like a fishing lake or a public park.
    Free-riderSomeone who enjoys a shared benefit without paying for it, because nobody can be stopped from using it.
    Government toolA way governments try to fix a spillover or shared-resource problem — a tax, a subsidy, a ban/rule, or providing something directly.
    Key Takeaways
    1. A decision can create a real cost or benefit for people who weren't part of making it — a spillover. 2. Shared, unowned resources can be overused and even collapse, even when no single user wants that outcome. 3. Public goods create a free-rider problem: people can enjoy the benefit without paying for it. 4. Governments have tools (tax, subsidy, ban, direct provision) to try to fix these problems — but every tool has trade-offs, as the candy-tax debate shows. 5. This lesson's ideas — felt through play, not lectured — are the intuitive seed for the formal externality and market-failure diagrams students will meet in Tier 2.
