describe why a shared, unowned resource can be overused, even when nobody individually wants that outcome

name at least two government tools to fix a spillover or protect a shared resource

Detailed Teaching Notes
Feel the unfairness before you name it
  • Hook: let students react to the factory-vs-village scenario before revealing anything formal — most will sense "that's not fair" without needing the word "externality."
  • Factory demo: clicking "Produce More" grows profit while draining river health — the factory's own books never show a cost for the river.
  • Free-rider streetlight experiment: if too few neighbors pay, the light never gets built; but once built, everyone gets it — including neighbors who never paid.
  • Fishing Lake game (companion file): fishing HIGH every round feels individually smart but can collapse the shared lake — a felt lesson in why shared resources need a rule or quota.
  • Candy-tax slider and debate: as the slider raises the tax, quantity bought falls — students pick a side and receive the counter-argument, modelling that real policy has trade-offs.

Worked Example
Story: "A small town shares one fishing lake. There are no rules about how many fish each boat can catch. Every boat owner reasons the same way: 'if I don't catch this fish now, another boat will.' By the third summer, the lake has almost no fish left, and every boat — even the most careful one — earns far less than in year one." Walk through why: no single boat "decided" to destroy the lake, but each boat's individually reasonable choice added up to a collective disaster — this is the tragedy of the commons, and it's exactly what the Fishing Lake game is built to let students discover for themselves.
Common Misconceptions
"If something is free to use, everyone will use it responsibly." Fix: point to the Fishing Lake game — nobody owning the resource is exactly why no single boat has a personal incentive to hold back, even when everyone would prefer the lake to survive.
"A spillover is always something bad." Fix: use the beekeeping and vaccination examples from the spectrum sort — a spillover can be a benefit nobody paid for, not just a cost nobody agreed to.
"The free-rider is doing something wrong/illegal." Fix: free-riding isn't cheating in a rule-breaking sense — it's a rational response to a good nobody can be excluded from. The "fix" is a policy tool (tax, direct provision), not blame.

Kid-Friendly Glossary
Spillover (externality)A cost or benefit from a decision that lands on someone who wasn't part of making it.
Shared resourceSomething everyone can use but nobody personally owns — like a fishing lake or a public park.
Free-riderSomeone who enjoys a shared benefit without paying for it, because nobody can be stopped from using it.
Government toolA way governments try to fix a spillover or shared-resource problem — a tax, a subsidy, a ban/rule, or providing something directly.
