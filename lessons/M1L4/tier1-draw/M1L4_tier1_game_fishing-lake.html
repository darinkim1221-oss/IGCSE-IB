The Fishing Lake: A Tragedy of the Commons
M1L4 · Tier 1 game — you share ONE lake with three rival boats nobody controls. Fish it hard and you catch more today — but if everyone fishes hard, the lake can collapse before the season ends. Fish smart, not just greedy.

Ages 10–12
Solo or pairs

🧠 For Tutors — Game Guide & Debrief
What this game teaches
The tragedy of the commons through direct consequence, not lecture. The lake is a shared, unowned resource (like the classic "shared bowl of goldfish crackers" version of this activity) — nobody's boat owns the fish until it's caught, so nobody has a personal incentive to hold back. A student who fishes hard every round catches more per round short-term but can wipe out the shared stock, cutting off catches (for themselves AND the rival boats) for the rest of the game. A student who fishes lightly protects the shared stock but earns less each round. There is no single "correct" effort — the lesson is that individually rational short-term choices can add up to a collectively bad outcome unless players adapt.
Core mechanic
Each of 6 rounds, the student picks a fishing effort — Low, Medium, or High — for their own boat. Three rival boats (simulated, not controlled by the student) always take a share of the CURRENT lake stock. After both the player and the rivals catch, the remaining stock regrows by a percentage — but only if enough fish are left to regrow from. If the stock drops too low in a single round, the lake collapses and the game ends early: no more catches for anyone, for the rest of the season.
Round structure
  • Briefing: the shared-lake setup is introduced — one lake, four boats, nobody owns the fish until it's caught.
  • Rounds 1–6: student picks Low / Medium / High effort each round, sees their catch, the rivals' catch, and the lake's remaining stock immediately, then confirms to move on.
  • Collapse check: if the stock falls too low after a round's catching, the lake collapses immediately and the game ends early with a "tragedy" ending.
  • Result: total fish caught by the player, whether the lake survived to the end of the season, and a tier reflecting both.
Pre-game brief (1 minute)
"This lake belongs to everyone and no one. Three other boats are fishing it too, and you can't stop them. Every round, decide how hard to fish — Low, Medium, or High. Fish hard and you catch more today, but the lake needs fish left over to regrow, or it collapses for everybody, including you, for the rest of the season."
Debrief questions
  • Did the lake survive all 6 rounds for you? If it collapsed, which round did it happen, and what effort were you using right before?
  • Was there ever a round where fishing HARD felt like the smart move, and a later round where fishing LIGHT felt smarter? What changed?
  • If you owned this lake personally — if it were YOUR private property — would you fish it differently than when it belonged to "everyone"? Why might that be?
Common misconceptions & fix
"Fishing High every round is always the winning strategy." Run it and watch the lake collapse by round 3 — the short-term gain doesn't survive contact with the shared, depleting resource. "The rival boats are the problem, not me." Point out that the player's own choices add to the SAME shared pool the rivals are drawing from — everyone's individually reasonable choice contributes to the same collective risk, which is the actual definition of the tragedy of the commons.
Variants
Simpler: play with a real shared bowl of goldfish crackers and 3-4 students each "fishing" (grabbing a handful) per round, refilling the bowl by a fixed amount between rounds — a tactile, unplugged version of this same game. Harder: introduce a "quota" rule mid-game (e.g., "starting round 4, no boat may take more than 15") and discuss why a government-style rule can fix a tragedy of the commons that individual choice alone could not.
