Extended Response · exam-style
11. Analyse whether a small independent bakery should merge with a rival bakery (horizontal merger) in order to reduce its average total cost. Structure your answer as CLAIM → EVIDENCE → LINK, referring to at least one relevant economic concept from this lesson (e.g. economies/diseconomies of scale, ATC, merger type, market power). [8]
Answer Key & Mark Scheme Notes
1. Average total cost is the cost of producing one typical unit of output — total cost divided by the quantity produced. Formula: ATC = TC ÷ Q. [1 mark for correct definition, 1 mark for correct formula]
2. AFC = FC ÷ Q = 400 ÷ 80 = $5.00. AVC = VC ÷ Q = 240 ÷ 80 = $3.00. ATC = TC ÷ Q = (400+240) ÷ 80 = 640 ÷ 80 = $8.00 (note ATC = AFC + AVC as a check: 5.00+3.00=8.00). [1 mark AFC correct with working, 1 mark AVC correct with working, 2 marks ATC correct with working (1 for method, 1 for final answer)]
3. TR = P × Q = 9.00 × 80 = $720. AR = TR ÷ Q = 720 ÷ 80 = $9.00 (always equals price). Profit = TR − TC = 720 − 640 = $80. [1 mark TR correct with working, 1 mark AR correct with working, 2 marks Profit correct with working]
4. A U-shaped curve: starts high at low Q, falls to a minimum point, then rises again. The falling (left) arm labelled "economies of scale," the lowest point labelled "Q(MES)" (minimum efficient scale) with a dashed line down to the quantity axis, and the rising (right) arm labelled "diseconomies of scale." [1 mark correct U-shape, 1 mark falling arm correctly labelled, 1 mark Q(MES) correctly identified, 1 mark rising arm correctly labelled]
5. An internal economy of scale arises from a single firm's own growth — e.g. a large supermarket chain negotiates bulk-buying discounts with suppliers that a small independent shop cannot access. An external economy of scale is available to EVERY firm in an industry because the whole industry has grown in one area — e.g. a tech hub where many software firms cluster together and all benefit from a shared pool of locally trained programmers. [2 marks internal, correctly defined + original example; 2 marks external, correctly defined + original example]
6. Horizontal → C (two rival film studios merge). Vertical → B (supermarket buys its milk supplier). Conglomerate → A (airline buys a hotel chain). [1 mark per correct match]
7. Cost per unit — Small: lower overheads and no layers of bureaucracy to fund. Large: economies of scale spread fixed costs over more output, lowering ATC. Flexibility — Small: can adapt quickly to local tastes or a changing trend. Large: can absorb short-term shocks and diversify across products/markets. Access to finance — Small: owner-financed, faster decisions, fewer approvals. Large: easier access to bank loans and outside investment due to size and track record. [1 mark per correct, reasonable cell — 6 cells total]
8. Price — Competitive: pushed toward the lowest sustainable level by rival sellers. Monopoly: can be set higher, limited mainly by what demand will bear. Choice/Variety — Competitive: high, as sellers differentiate to attract customers. Monopoly: low — one seller, take it or leave it. Long-run profit — Competitive: competed down toward normal levels as new sellers enter if profit is attractive. Monopoly: can persist above normal levels because barriers to entry block rival firms from competing it away. [2 marks per feature: 1 for correct competitive-market side, 1 for correct monopoly side]
9. Most to least competitive: Perfect competition → Monopolistic competition → Oligopoly → Monopoly. [1 mark per correctly placed structure, or full marks for the fully correct order]
10. The seller knows the car's true condition, but the buyer cannot verify it before purchase — this is asymmetric information. Because buyers can't distinguish good cars from bad ones, they will only be willing to pay an AVERAGE price reflecting the mix of quality in the whole market. This average price undervalues genuinely good cars, so owners of good cars are unwilling to sell at that price and exit the market, while owners of bad cars are happy to sell at (or above) their car's true worth. Over time, this can leave the market dominated by lower-quality cars — a market failure driven entirely by the information gap, not by cost or price controls. [1 mark correct definition of asymmetric information applied to the scenario, 1 mark explaining the average-price mechanism, 2 marks explaining how good sellers are driven out, leaving a market of "lemons"]
