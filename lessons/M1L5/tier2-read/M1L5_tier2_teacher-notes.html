Detailed Teaching Notes
From calculation to diagram to market structure
  • Hook: "profit isn't a mystery number — it's what's left after every cost of production is subtracted from every unit of revenue." Frame the lesson as building a calculation toolkit, not memorising definitions.
  • Command-term drill: run Define / Calculate / Compare before the calculator appears — "calculate" demands formula + substitution + labelled final answer, not just a number.
  • Cost & revenue live calculator: drag the quantity slider and watch TC, AFC, AVC, ATC, TR, AR, and profit recalculate together. Insist students verify ATC = AFC + AVC as a self-check.
  • Economies/diseconomies of scale tabs: four cells students blur together — internal economies (a firm's OWN bulk-buying/technical/managerial/financial advantages), internal diseconomies (communication/coordination problems inside one oversized firm), external economies (advantages available to EVERY firm because a whole industry clustered in one area), external diseconomies (congestion, rising local wages/rents as a whole industry over-grows). Test the internal/external distinction explicitly.
  • ATC diagram: drill the sequence every time — draw a U-shaped curve against quantity, label the falling arm as economies of scale, mark the lowest point as Q(MES) with a dashed line to the axis, label the rising arm as diseconomies of scale. The most common mark-loss is a straight downward line instead of a genuine U-shape.
  • Firms, mergers & markets tabs: five tabs in one slide — objectives (profit max/growth/survival/social welfare), types of firms (sector, ownership, small-vs-large advantages), mergers (horizontal/vertical/conglomerate with real cases), competitive vs. monopoly (qualitative only — no diagrams at IGCSE), and firms & production (derived demand, labour- vs. capital-intensive, production vs. productivity). Push students to justify each classification by reasoning about the relationship between the firms, not by memorising the example.
Visuals & Diagrams
📉
The ATC diagram: a single U-shaped curve against quantity — falling arm (economies of scale), a labelled minimum point Q(MES), rising arm (diseconomies of scale). This is the lesson's one required diagram, newly explicit at IGCSE 2027–2029.
Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "A small bakery is considering a horizontal merger with a rival bakery to reduce its average total cost. Explain whether this is likely to succeed." Model: (1) identify where the bakery currently sits on its ATC curve — if still on the FALLING arm (below Q(MES)), merging can genuinely lower ATC by spreading fixed costs; (2) if already AT or PAST Q(MES), merging risks pushing combined output into the RISING arm (diseconomies of scale); (3) conclude the answer is conditional on the firm's current position on the ATC curve, not automatic — the exact CLAIM → EVIDENCE → LINK structure required in workbook Q6.
Common Misconceptions
"A bigger firm always has lower average costs." Fix: point to the rising arm of the ATC diagram — diseconomies of scale are real and examinable. Growth only lowers ATC up to Q(MES).
"AR and Price are different numbers." Fix: for a firm selling all output at one price, AR = TR ÷ Q = (P × Q) ÷ Q = P — they are numerically identical.
"A monopoly can charge literally any price it wants." Fix: a monopolist has real pricing power, but customers can still refuse to buy — demand still constrains the profit-maximising price.

Exam-Precise Glossary
Total cost (TC)Fixed costs plus variable costs — the full cost of producing a given output. TC = FC + VC.
Average total cost (ATC)The cost of producing one typical unit — TC ÷ Q. Falls (economies of scale) then rises (diseconomies of scale) as output grows.
Economies of scaleFalling average cost as output/size increases, up to the minimum efficient scale (Q(MES)).
Diseconomies of scaleRising average cost as output/size increases beyond Q(MES), often from management/coordination problems.
Merger (horizontal/vertical/conglomerate)Two firms joining into one — same product/stage (horizontal), same supply chain different stage (vertical), or unrelated industries (conglomerate).
MonopolyA market with a single dominant seller, able to sustain a higher price and offer less choice than a competitive market.
