Learning Objectives
By the end, students can…
explain why hiring more workers can make each item cheaper to produce (economies of scale), and why that can reverse past a certain size (diseconomies of scale)
classify a business by sector (primary, secondary, tertiary) and by ownership (private, public)
distinguish horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate mergers using concrete examples
describe how price and choice change when a market has only ONE seller (a monopoly) compared to many sellers
give one reason growing a business helps, and one reason growing too much can hurt

Detailed Teaching Notes
Feel the curve before you name it
  • Hook: "hiring more bakers makes each cookie cheaper... so should a factory just keep hiring forever?" — let students guess before revealing anything. Most instinctively say "yes, more workers = better," which sets up the reversal.
  • Hire-a-baker live demo: each click hires one more baker — average cost per cookie falls, bottoms out, then rises again as a "factory floor" crowding meter fills. Pause at the turning point and ask "why would MORE workers make things more expensive per cookie?" before revealing the answer.
  • Firm sort: classify real businesses by sector and ownership — include a public-sector tertiary example alongside a private one, since students often default to assuming "tertiary = private."
  • Merger sort: students practice telling horizontal, vertical, and conglomerate mergers apart by reasoning about the relationship between the two firms (same product? same supply chain? unrelated?) rather than memorising a definition cold.
  • Lemonade monopoly role play: students personally raise the price of the only lemonade stand in town and watch customers drop off — a felt introduction to a monopolist's pricing power and its limits.
  • Cookie Factory game (companion file): a scored, round-based version of the live demo with a merger event partway through — accepting can push the player into the efficient zone OR into an overcrowded, loss-making one, reinforcing that a merger is just another way to change size, and bigger isn't automatically better.

Worked Example
Story: "A one-baker cookie stall sells slowly and each cookie costs a lot to make, because the oven and the rent are being paid for by almost nobody. The owner hires four more bakers — suddenly the oven is always full and each cookie gets much cheaper to bake. Excited, the owner keeps hiring… but by baker number twelve, the kitchen is so crowded that bakers are bumping into each other, ovens are double-booked, and mistakes pile up. Each cookie is now MORE expensive to make than it was at baker number five." Walk through why: the first few hires spread fixed costs (economies of scale); the later hires create crowding costs that outweigh the savings (diseconomies of scale).
Common Misconceptions
"Hiring more workers always makes a business more efficient." Fix: point to the live demo's crowding meter and the moment average cost starts climbing again — efficiency gains from size have a limit, past which the same growth becomes a cost.
"A merger is automatically a good idea because the company gets bigger." Fix: use the Cookie Factory game's merger event — accepting when the factory is already near its efficient size can push it straight into an overcrowded, loss-making zone.
"If there's only one seller, they can charge literally any price they want." Fix: use the lemonade role play — raise the price too far and customers walk away entirely. A monopolist has real pricing power, but it isn't unlimited.

Kid-Friendly Glossary
Economies of scaleWhen growing bigger makes each item cheaper to produce, because fixed costs are spread over more output.
Diseconomies of scaleWhen growing TOO big makes each item more expensive again, often due to crowding or harder management.
MergerWhen two firms join together to become one. Horizontal = same product; Vertical = same supply chain, different stage; Conglomerate = unrelated businesses.
MonopolyA market with only ONE seller, who has more power over price than a seller in a market with many competitors.
