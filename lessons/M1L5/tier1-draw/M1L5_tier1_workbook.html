Warm-up
Show what you discovered today
1. In the Cookie Factory game, what happened to the average cost per cookie as you hired your FIRST few bakers? Why?
2. Fill in the blank: when hiring more workers makes each item CHEAPER to make, economists call this ______________ of scale.
3. What happened in the game once the factory got TOO crowded? What is that pattern called?
4. Match each business to its correct SECTOR.
A wheat farm____________
A cookie factory____________
A hair salon____________
Choices: Primary · Secondary · Tertiary
5. True or False: a business owned and run by the government (like a public library) is a "private" firm.
6. Two rival cookie factories join together to become one bigger cookie factory. What TYPE of merger is this — horizontal, vertical, or conglomerate? Explain your choice.
7. A cookie factory buys the flour mill that supplies its flour. What TYPE of merger is this? Explain your choice.
8. In the lemonade stand game, what happened to the price — and to how many customers stayed — as the stand became the ONLY seller in town?
9. In the used-bike game, why couldn't you tell just by looking whether a bike was "peachy" or a "lemon"? What is it called when a seller knows more about a product than the buyer does?
10. Do you think a factory should ALWAYS try to grow as big as possible? Give one reason why growing can help, and one reason why growing too much can hurt.
🏠 Family Discussion Mission
Ask a family member: "Have you ever bought something from a business that turned out to be the ONLY place selling that thing nearby? What was that experience like — price, choice, quality?" Write down their example and say whether it sounds like it came from a competitive market or a monopoly.
Answer Key
1. It fell — each new baker helped spread the cost of the oven and rent over more cookies, so the average cost per cookie went down. This is economies of scale.
2. Economies. — Economies of scale means growing bigger makes each unit cheaper to produce.
3. Average cost per cookie started rising again as bakers got in each other's way on a crowded floor. This is called diseconomies of scale.
4. Wheat farm: Primary. Cookie factory: Secondary. Hair salon: Tertiary.
5. False. — A government-owned business is a "public" firm, not a "private" one. Private firms are owned by individuals or shareholders.
6. Horizontal merger. — Both factories make the SAME product at the SAME stage of production (baking cookies), which is the definition of a horizontal merger.
7. Vertical merger. — The factory is joining with a business at a DIFFERENT stage of the same supply chain (a supplier), which is the definition of a vertical merger.
8. The price kept rising, and fewer customers stayed willing to buy as it got more expensive — some walked away thirsty rather than pay the higher price. This is the power a monopolist (the only seller) has over price that a seller in a competitive market doesn't have.
9. You couldn't tell from the outside because the SELLER knows things about the bike's real condition that the BUYER can't see just by looking. This is called asymmetric information — one side of a deal knows more than the other.
10. Accept any reasonable pair, e.g. Growing helps: "spreading fixed costs like rent and machinery over more products makes each one cheaper (economies of scale)." Growing too much hurts: "past a certain size, a crowded factory floor or hard-to-manage workforce can make average cost rise again (diseconomies of scale)."
