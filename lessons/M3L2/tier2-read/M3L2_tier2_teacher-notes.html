Detailed Teaching Notes
Full syllabus rigor — every tool, every reason, one diagram
  • Command-term drill: run Define / Explain / Comment first — "comment" previews the balanced reasoning needed for the extended response.
  • Tools of protection: five tabs including embargoes (newly explicit at IGCSE 2027-29) and a LOW-detail administrative-barriers tab — one example is sufficient there, don't expand further.
  • Reasons for restriction: the FULL eight-item IGCSE list, three more than Tier 1 (anti-dumping, reducing a current-account deficit, raising tax revenue). Students should be able to match a real scenario to the correct named reason.
  • The tariff diagram: drill the sequence every time — draw D and S, mark the world price BELOW domestic equilibrium (this is an importing country), label the import gap, then add the tariff price line and show all four quantities (Qs before/after, Qd before/after). The toggle to "+ Welfare Areas" is the IB-level extension — walk through each of the four shaded regions by name before revealing which one is deadweight loss.
  • Consequences tabs: push students past "the home country benefits" — trading partners genuinely lose export sales, and retaliation is a real historical pattern (trade wars).
Visuals & Diagrams

The tariff diagram is the key visual for this lesson — a domestic demand-and-supply diagram with a world price line, a post-tariff price line, and the resulting import gap. The welfare-areas version (consumer surplus loss, producer surplus gain, government revenue, deadweight loss) is explicitly IB-level detail, layered on top of the same base diagram via the toggle — IGCSE 0455 requires only the basic version.
Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "Discuss whether a government should use a tariff to protect a declining domestic industry." Model: CLAIM — a tariff can be justified as a short-term measure but carries real costs. EVIDENCE — winners: domestic workers in the declining industry keep their jobs longer, and the government may gain tariff revenue; losers: consumers face higher prices and reduced choice, and trading partners lose export sales and may retaliate. LINK — a temporary, well-targeted tariff giving the industry time to restructure may be justified, but a permanent tariff that never lets the industry adapt likely costs consumers more than it's worth long-term. This is the exact structure required in workbook Q6.
Common Misconceptions
"A tariff and a quota do the same thing." Fix: a tariff works through PRICE (raising it), a quota works through a fixed QUANTITY limit — the actual quantity imported under a tariff still depends on how buyers respond to the new price.
"Deadweight loss is the whole shaded area between the two price lines." Fix: use the welfare-areas toggle — deadweight loss is specifically the two SMALL triangles; the rest of the shaded region is accounted for as consumer loss, producer gain, or government revenue.
"Only the home country is affected by its own tariff." Fix: use the Consequences tabs — trading partners lose export sales and can retaliate, which is how trade disputes escalate.

Exam-Precise Glossary
TariffA tax placed on imported goods.
Import quotaA limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported.
EmbargoA complete ban on trade with a country or of a good.
Consumer surplus lossThe value consumers lose as the price they pay rises due to a tariff.
Producer surplus gainThe extra value domestic producers gain from selling more at the higher price.
Deadweight lossThe loss of overall economic efficiency created by a tariff, captured by no group.
