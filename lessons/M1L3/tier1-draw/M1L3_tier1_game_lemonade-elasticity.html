Lemonade Elasticity Challenge
M1L3 · Tier 1 game — you run TWO stalls at the same fair: Candy Corner (lots of nearby competitors) and Desert Water (the only water for miles). Pick a price for each, every round, and chase the highest total revenue.

🧠 For Tutors — Game Guide & Debrief
What this game teaches
PED intuition through consequence, not formula. Running two stalls side by side — one with many substitutes (Candy Corner) and one with none (Desert Water) — lets a student discover, by trial and error, that the SAME pricing move ("raise the price") makes money for one stall and loses money for the other. No elasticity number is ever shown; the game is won by noticing the pattern.
Core mechanic
Each of 4 rounds, the student sets a price ($1–$4) for BOTH stalls independently. Quantity sold reacts differently for each: Candy Corner's sales collapse fast as price rises (many substitutes = stretchy demand); Desert Water's sales fall only slightly (no substitute = stiff demand). Revenue is tallied per stall and totalled across all rounds.
Round structure
  • Briefing: two-stall scenario is introduced with the "why" for each (competitors vs. no substitute).
  • Rounds 1–4: student sets both prices, sees quantity sold and revenue per stall immediately, then confirms to move to the next round.
  • Result: total revenue across both stalls compared against the best possible total, with a tier and a plain-language explanation of what pricing pattern won.
Pre-game brief (1 minute)
"You're running two stalls at the same fair. Candy Corner has ten other candy stalls right next to it. Desert Water is the only water stall for fifty miles. You set the price for each, every round — try to make as much total money as you can, and watch closely: does raising the price always help?"
Debrief questions
  • What happened to Candy Corner's revenue when you pushed the price too high? Why do you think that happened there and not at Desert Water?
  • Which stall could you push to a much higher price without losing many buyers? What made that possible?
  • If you owned a real shop, how would you decide whether raising your price is a good idea?
Common misconceptions & fix
"Raising the price always makes more money." Point to the Candy Corner revenue numbers at $3–$4 — sales collapse faster than the price rises, so total revenue falls. "Both stalls should be priced the same way." Contrast the optimal price found for each stall — the right pricing strategy depends on how many substitutes buyers have, not on a single rule.
Variants
Simpler: run Candy Corner only, across more rounds, to isolate one pattern at a time. Harder: add a mid-game twist — "a new candy stall just opened next door" (Candy Corner becomes even more stretchy) or "the desert water truck broke down" (Desert Water becomes even stiffer) — and ask students to re-optimise.
