Warm-up
Show what you discovered today
1. In your own words, what does it mean for a good to be "stretchy" (in economics: elastic)?
2. Fill in the blank: A good is usually "stiff" (inelastic) when it has ______________ substitutes.
3. Why does petrol's price swing around a lot, but a haircut's price barely moves? Explain in 1–2 sentences.
4. In the Lemonade Elasticity Challenge, which stall could you raise the price on WITHOUT losing many buyers — Candy Corner or Desert Water? Why?
5. True or False: Raising the price always makes a seller more money.
6. Match each product to STRETCHY or STIFF.
One brand of chips (many others nearby)____________
Insulin (life-saving medicine, no substitute)____________
Salt (tiny part of your budget, no real substitute)____________
A trendy toy with 5 similar copycats____________
7. Name TWO reasons a good might end up being "stretchy" (easy to give up).
8. A shop owner raises the price of a stretchy good a lot and total revenue FALLS. Explain why this happens, using the word "buyers" in your answer.
9. If you owned a real shop, name ONE product you'd feel safe raising the price on, and ONE you would NOT. Explain your reasoning.
10. How could a government use the idea of "stretchy vs stiff" to change what people buy (for example, to get people to smoke less or drive less)?
🏠 Family Discussion Mission
Ask a family member: "Is there a product you'd keep buying almost no matter how much the price went up? What about one you'd drop the moment it got even a little more expensive?" Write down their two examples and why.
Answer Key
1. Accept any answer capturing: buyers react a lot to a price change — if the price goes up even a little, a lot of people stop buying (usually because there are other choices).
2. Few / no. — With no other option, buyers keep buying even when price rises.
3. Sample: "Petrol has few substitutes in the short run (you still need to drive), but people can often postpone or skip a haircut, or few substitutes exist for driving vs. many barbers compete for haircuts, keeping that price steady." Accept reasoning built around substitutes/necessity.
4. Desert Water — because it has no substitute nearby, buyers keep paying even at a high price. Candy Corner's buyers flee to a competitor the moment price rises.
5. False. — For a stretchy good, raising price too much can cause so many buyers to leave that total revenue actually falls.
6. Chips: Stretchy. Insulin: Stiff. Salt: Stiff. Trendy toy with copycats: Stretchy.
7. Accept any two: lots of substitutes exist, it's a luxury not a need, it takes up a big chunk of your money, you have time to shop around.
8. Accept: because so many buyers switch to a substitute or stop buying that the number of buyers falls by MORE than the price rose, so price × quantity (revenue) goes down overall.
9. Accept any reasonable pair with correct reasoning tied to substitutes/necessity (e.g. "raise price on my only ice-cream stand at the one beach" vs. "not on a candy brand next to five others").
10. Sample: "A government could raise the price of cigarettes a LOT, because even though smokers may not quit easily at first (stiff in the short run), a big enough price rise over time can push behaviour, especially for stretchier related choices like buying a second car or driving for short trips." Accept any answer connecting a price change to a stretchy/stiff good to encourage or discourage a behaviour.
