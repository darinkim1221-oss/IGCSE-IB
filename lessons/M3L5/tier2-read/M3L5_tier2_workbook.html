Answer Key & Mark Scheme Notes
1. Economic growth is an increase in a country's total output over time (usually measured by % change in real GDP). Economic development is a broader, multidimensional improvement in people's well-being — including health, education, and income — not just how much is produced. [2 marks per correct, clearly distinguished definition]
2. Since GDP per person is the same but HDI differs, Country A must be doing better on the OTHER components of HDI — likely higher life expectancy and/or more years of schooling than Country B. This shows that income alone does not determine development; how that income translates into health and education outcomes matters too. [2 marks correct reasoning, 2 marks correct conclusion about non-income factors]
3. Advantage (accept any reasonable, e.g.): 4,000 new jobs directly reduce local unemployment and raise household incomes. Disadvantage (accept any reasonable, e.g.): local competitor businesses closing could offset some of the job gains, and the increased pollution risk could impose environmental and health costs on the surrounding community. [2 marks per correctly analysed point, with an explained mechanism, not just a listed fact]
4. Low income means the country has little left over after meeting basic needs, so savings and investment stay low; low investment means little new capital or infrastructure is built, keeping productivity low; low productivity means output (and therefore income) stays low — completing the loop back to where it started. Without an outside injection of investment, aid, or a policy shock, there is no obvious way to break out of the cycle using only the country's own limited resources. [4 marks for a correctly sequenced explanation of the full loop]
5. Income distribution (accept any reasonable, e.g.): the gains from globalisation have not been shared evenly — some workers and regions benefit hugely while others, especially in industries facing new low-cost competition, lose out. Environment (accept any reasonable, e.g.): moving goods and materials globally, and rising production to meet worldwide demand, has increased energy use and carbon emissions. [2 marks per correctly explained effect]
6. Band 3 (6–8 marks): CLAIM (e.g. "free trade can promote development but is not sufficient on its own"); EVIDENCE (free trade gives countries access to larger export markets and can drive specialisation gains, potentially raising incomes and funding further development — as seen in export-led growth strategies; however, countries with weak infrastructure or institutions may struggle to compete even with open access to trade, and some domestic industries can be harmed by import competition before new export industries develop); LINK (a supported conclusion such as "free trade is likely to help development most when paired with investment in infrastructure, education and institutions — trade openness ALONE does not guarantee development"). Band 2 (3–5): explains free trade's effect but no clear real-world example or final judgement. Band 1 (1–2): a one-sided assertion with no mechanism or evidence.
