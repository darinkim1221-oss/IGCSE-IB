Extended Response · mock exam · Evaluate
11. Evaluate whether a monopoly is always harmful to consumers. Structure your answer as CLAIM → ANALYSIS → COUNTER → JUDGEMENT, referring to at least THREE concepts from this module (e.g. price, choice/variety, economies of scale, barriers to entry, government intervention). [8]
Answer Key & Mark Scheme Notes
1. A — Movement along the demand curve. [1 mark]
2. B — Price floor. [1 mark]
3. A — Horizontal merger. [1 mark]
4. Opportunity cost: the next best alternative given up when a choice is made. Quantity demanded: the specific amount of a good buyers are willing and able to buy at a given price. "Quantity demanded" (not just "demand") is used because "demand" refers to the WHOLE relationship/curve between price and quantity, while a price change only moves buyers along that same curve — using "demand" for both would blur a movement with a shift. [1 mark each definition, 1 mark for the precision explanation]
5. It is imprecise because it implies the whole demand relationship ("demand") has changed, when in fact only the QUANTITY DEMANDED has changed in response to a price movement along the same curve. Correct version: "When the price goes up, the quantity demanded goes down (a movement along the demand curve)." [1 mark for identifying the shift/movement confusion, 1 mark for a correctly worded rewrite]
6. (a) % change in price = (2.40−2.00)/2.00 × 100 = +20%. % change in quantity = (176−200)/200 × 100 = −12%. (b) PED = % change in Qd ÷ % change in P = −12 ÷ 20 = −0.6 (magnitude 0.6). (c) Since the magnitude is less than 1, demand for cupcakes is inelastic. [1 mark %ΔP, 1 mark %ΔQd, 2 marks PED with correct method + answer, 1 mark correct elastic/inelastic classification consistent with the student's own PED value]
7. The $0.50 tax raises the bakery's cost per cupcake sold (a tax is a cost the firm must pay per unit, just like the Market Day sales-tax event). The bakery passed on $0.40 of the $0.50 tax to customers (price rose by $0.40, from $2.00 to $2.40) and absorbed the remaining $0.10 itself — since demand is inelastic, the bakery could pass on MOST of the tax without losing many customers, so consumers end up bearing the larger share of the burden. [2 marks correctly explaining the cost increase, 2 marks correctly reasoning from the data that consumers bear more of the burden because demand is inelastic]
8. Government reason (accept any reasonable, e.g.): raising revenue to fund public services, or discouraging excessive sugar consumption for public health. Bakery owner objection (accept any reasonable, e.g.): the tax raises their costs and, even though they passed most of it on, they still lost some sales (200→176) and absorbed part of the cost themselves, squeezing profit. [2 marks per reason: 1 for a valid reason, 1 for linking it explicitly to the data/scenario]
9. Accept any THREE of: (1) define what a price ceiling is precisely, rather than assuming the reader knows; (2) add ANALYSIS — explain the mechanism (a ceiling below equilibrium price causes excess demand/a shortage, since landlords supply less at the lower price while more tenants want housing); (3) add a COUNTER — some tenants benefit (those who get housing at the lower price) while others are worse off (those who can't find any housing at all due to the shortage, or face lower-quality maintained units); (4) end with a JUDGEMENT that is CONDITIONAL rather than absolute (e.g. "it helps SOME tenants who secure housing, but may harm others through reduced availability — so it is not simply 'good'"). [1 mark per valid, specific improvement named — must describe what Band 1 is missing, not just restate the answer]
10. Accept any answer with a clear conditional judgement, e.g.: "Price controls help consumers most when the shortage/surplus they create is small relative to the fairness or affordability gain, but harm consumers when the resulting shortage (ceiling) or surplus (floor) is severe enough to outweigh that benefit." [1 mark clear judgement stated, 1 mark judgement is genuinely conditional (not a flat yes/no), 1 mark condition named is economically valid]
11. Band 3 (6–8 marks): CLAIM (e.g. "a monopoly is not automatically harmful — its effect on consumers depends on how it uses its market power"); ANALYSIS (a monopoly can charge a higher price and offer less choice than a competitive market, since there is no rival forcing price down or variety up — link to price/choice concepts); COUNTER (a monopoly can also achieve economies of scale that a fragmented competitive market cannot, potentially lowering costs and, if the government regulates it or a price ceiling is imposed, prices too; some monopolies invest heavily in innovation because guaranteed long-run profit funds R&D); JUDGEMENT (a supported conclusion such as "a monopoly is harmful when barriers to entry are high and it is unregulated, but can benefit consumers when its scale economies are passed on or intervention constrains its pricing power"). Band 2 (3–5): names relevant concepts (price, choice, economies of scale) but the four-part structure is incomplete — e.g. no real counter-argument, or a judgement that is just a restated claim. Band 1 (1–2): a one-sided assertion ("monopolies are bad because they charge too much") with no named economic concept, no counter, and no conditional judgement.
