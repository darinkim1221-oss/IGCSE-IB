Detailed Teaching Notes
From recall to judgement
Open with the hook (slide s0): two students who know exactly the same facts can still earn very different marks — the gap is HOW they write, not WHAT they know. This reframes the whole lesson as an exam-craft lesson, not a content-review lesson, and should be stated to students explicitly so they don't expect new economics.
The command-term drill (slide s1) separates discuss (present more than one side), evaluate (present more than one side AND weigh them to reach a judgement), and to what extent (state a degree/condition, not a flat yes/no) — these three are consistently the most conflated command terms across both boards. Have students physically sort past question stems into the three buckets before moving on.
The MCQ warm-up (slide s2) is deliberately drawn from across the whole module (elasticity, intervention, mergers) — treat wrong answers as a diagnostic for which earlier lesson needs a 2-minute verbal recap, not just "the correct answer is X."
The short-answer mark-scheme literacy slide (s3) uses an ATC calculation to make a single point explicit: marks are awarded for the FORMULA, the SUBSTITUTION, and the labelled FINAL ANSWER as three separate credit-bearing steps — a bare correct number with no visible method line can lose most of the marks even when it happens to be right.
The data-response practice slide (s4) rehearses the (a)/(b)/(c) escalating-depth structure common to both boards: (a) is usually a calculation or definition, (b) an explanation using the data, (c) a short discuss/evaluate grounded in that same data — model reading the WHOLE question stem before writing any answer, since (c) often needs a claim that (a) and (b) already support.
The levels-of-response slide (s5) is this lesson's IGCSE-adapter centrepiece: put a genuine Band 1 answer and a genuine Band 3 answer to the SAME 8-mark question side by side and have students name the differences themselves before you reveal the mark-scheme language (structure / content / judgement). The workbook's Q9 repeats this exercise for independent practice — the skill being taught is diagnostic (spot what's missing), not just descriptive (know what Band 3 looks like).
The evaluation paragraph slide (s6) walks CLAIM → ANALYSIS → COUNTER → JUDGEMENT using a bakery-merger example already familiar from M1L5. Emphasise that the COUNTER is the step students skip most often under time pressure — without it, the JUDGEMENT has nothing to weigh and collapses into a restated claim.
The IB "discuss" structure slide (s7) is explicitly a first rehearsal of the mini Part (b) format: two genuinely distinct sides, then a supported judgement in one or two sentences — NOT a full IB-style essay. Keep the modelled example short enough that students can produce a comparable one in 3–4 minutes.
The misconception clinic (slide s8) is deliberately re-framed around "why this specific wording costs marks" rather than "why this is wrong" — e.g. writing "demand falls" instead of "quantity demanded falls" is not just imprecise, an examiner may read it as confusing a shift with a movement and withhold the mark even if the student's underlying reasoning was correct.
The timing & strategy slide (s9) sets three durable habits: roughly one minute per mark as a pacing check; a 30–60 second plan (bullet the claim/analysis/counter/judgement) before writing any extended response; and letting the command word set the depth (a "state" question needs one line, an "evaluate" question needs the full four-part structure) so students stop either under- or over-writing relative to the marks on offer.
The concept connections recap (slide s10) closes the loop by explicitly re-walking L1–L2, L3–L4, and L5 as three connected movements in one story, immediately before the Exam Adapter Slot — this is the direct lead-in to workbook Q1-style whole-module synthesis questions.

Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "Evaluate whether a monopoly is always harmful to consumers." Model: CLAIM — a monopoly is not automatically harmful; its effect depends on how it uses its market power. ANALYSIS — a monopoly can charge a higher price and offer less choice than a competitive market (link to price/choice, M1L5). COUNTER — a monopoly can also achieve economies of scale a fragmented market cannot, and if regulated (a price ceiling, M1L4) some of that cost saving can reach consumers; guaranteed long-run profit can also fund innovation. JUDGEMENT — harmful when barriers to entry are high and unregulated; less harmful, potentially beneficial, when scale economies are passed on or intervention constrains pricing power. This is the exact structure required in workbook Q11.
Common Misconceptions
"Discuss and evaluate mean the same thing — just write everything I know." Fix: discuss only requires presenting more than one side; evaluate additionally requires weighing those sides to reach a supported judgement. Writing everything you know without structure earns Band 1–2 regardless of command term.
"Price rises so demand falls" (written in an exam answer). Fix: this describes a MOVEMENT along the demand curve — the correct phrase is "quantity demanded falls." An examiner may read the imprecise version as a genuine shift/movement confusion and withhold the mark.
"Cost and price are the same word for the same thing." Fix: cost is what it takes a FIRM to produce something; price is what a BUYER pays. Conflating them collapses profit-related reasoning (profit = price − cost) into circular, unmarkable statements.
Exam Adapter Slot
Source: Wiki §7 drip map, row M1 L6 ("Evaluation")
Skill focus this lesson: first full evaluation paragraph — claim → analysis → counter → judgement. Command terms in play: discuss, evaluate, to what extent.
IGCSE
Levels-of-response introduction: Band 1 vs. Band 3 on a (d)-style question worth 8 marks; developing "the examiner's eye" — the ability to read a mark scheme and predict where a given answer would land before it is graded.
Practice move: give students a genuine Band 1 answer and have them rewrite it into Band 3 by adding exactly the ingredients it's missing (definition, mechanism/analysis, a counter-consideration, a conditional judgement) — this is workbook Q9's exact exercise.
IB
First introduction to AO3: the "discuss" structure — two genuinely distinct sides plus a supported judgement — rehearsed as a first mini Part (b) response, not a full essay.
Practice move: give students a "to what extent" stem and ask for ONE sentence containing a clear, conditional judgement (workbook Q10) before ever asking for a full paragraph — build the judgement-writing muscle in isolation first.
Extension Activities
  • Mark-scheme swap: in pairs, each student writes a short answer to the same 8-mark question, then swaps and marks their partner's answer against the Band 1/2/3 descriptors, justifying the band awarded in writing.
  • Command-term audit: collect five real exam-style question stems from earlier lessons in this module and sort them by command term, then predict the expected response length/depth for each before checking against the mark scheme.
  • [IB] Judgement bank: build a personal bank of five "conditional judgement" sentence starters (e.g. "This is beneficial when… but harmful when…") that can be reused across different "to what extent" questions all year.

    Exam-Precise Glossary
    Command termThe instruction word in a question (e.g. define, explain, discuss, evaluate) that fixes exactly what kind of response is required and how much depth earns credit.
    Levels of responseAn IGCSE mark-scheme format that grades an extended answer holistically into bands (e.g. Band 1–3) based on structure, content, and judgement, rather than awarding isolated single marks.
    Claim → Analysis → Counter → JudgementThe four-part scaffold for a full evaluation paragraph: a clear position, the reasoning/mechanism behind it, a genuine opposing consideration, and a final supported (usually conditional) conclusion.
    "Discuss" (IB)Requires presenting more than one side of an issue; does not by itself require a final judgement (that is "evaluate" or "to what extent").
    "To what extent" (IB)Requires a judgement stated as a DEGREE or CONDITION, not a flat yes/no — e.g. "largely… but only when…".
    Movement vs. shiftA price change causes a movement ALONG a demand or supply curve; a change in any other factor (income, tastes, related-good prices) causes the whole curve to SHIFT.
    Key Takeaways
    1. This lesson teaches exam CRAFT, not new economics — every concept used here should already be familiar from M1L1–M1L5. 2. Command terms fix the required depth of response; conflating discuss/evaluate/to what extent is the single most common source of lost marks at this level. 3. The levels-of-response format (IGCSE) rewards structure, content, AND judgement holistically — not a checklist of isolated facts. 4. CLAIM → ANALYSIS → COUNTER → JUDGEMENT is the reusable scaffold for every extended response for the rest of the course; the COUNTER step is the one students skip most under time pressure. 5. The IB "discuss" mini Part (b) introduced here is a first, small rehearsal — calibrate expectations to first exposure, not a full-length essay.
