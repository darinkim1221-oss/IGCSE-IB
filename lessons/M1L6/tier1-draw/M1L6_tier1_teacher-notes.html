Learning Objectives
By the end, students can…
retell the whole micro story of this module — scarcity → prices → elasticity → government → firms — as one connected narrative, not five separate topics
correctly distinguish a MOVEMENT ALONG a demand curve (caused by a price change) from a SHIFT of the whole curve (caused by something else, like income or tastes)
use "quantity demanded falls" rather than "demand falls" when describing the effect of a price rise
explain the difference between COST (what it takes a firm to make something) and PRICE (what a buyer pays)
identify which government rule — tax, subsidy, price ceiling, or price floor — is at play in a scenario, and predict its effect on sellers and buyers
describe what changes for a seller and for buyers when one seller becomes the only seller (a monopoly)
connect classic module vocabulary (opportunity cost, equilibrium, elasticity, externality, economies of scale) to the Key Concepts of Efficiency, Choice, and Interdependence

Detailed Teaching Notes
Connect it, don't re-teach it
Open with the hook — "you've explored six big ideas this module... can you connect them into ONE story?" — and resist the urge to answer it yourself. This lesson works best when students do the connecting, with you filling gaps rather than delivering fresh content.
The Story Map (slide s1) is a five-chapter recap — Scarcity & Choice, Demand & Supply, Elasticity, Government & Market Failure, Firms & Scale — each a single tap-to-reveal card. Use it as a cold-call springboard: reveal a chapter, then ask a student to add one example from their own life before moving on.
The Concept Connection matching (slide s2) is the heart of the synthesis work — five real-feeling mini-scenes, each mapping to a concept from a DIFFERENT lesson (inelastic demand, a price ceiling, economies of scale, a horizontal merger, an externality). This is deliberately scenario-first: students reason from the situation to the vocabulary, which is the harder and more valuable direction.
The Misconception Clinic (slide s3) targets the three traps named explicitly in the course spec: confusing a demand SHIFT with a movement along the curve, the sloppy phrase "price rises so demand falls" (it's quantity demanded, not demand, that falls), and conflating cost with price. Do not rush this slide — these three mix-ups are the most common source of lost marks once students reach Tier 2 exam-style writing.
The Key Concept Sort (slide s4) asks students to file eight familiar terms under Efficiency, Choice, or Interdependence. Some placements are debatable by design (e.g. a merger could arguably serve efficiency OR market power) — use disagreement as a discussion trigger, not something to smooth over.
The quiz-show buzzer bonus round (slide s5) is a fast true/false consolidation spanning the whole module — read each statement aloud dramatically and let students "buzz in" verbally before tapping an answer, to recreate the team quiz-show energy the course spec calls for.
Slide s6 bridges into the standalone Market Day game (companion file) — the capstone simulation where students run a stall across six market days, each with a different government rule, ending in a Monopoly Licence auction. Run it as its own session (15–20 minutes): it is a full review of government intervention (L4) and market power (L5) wrapped in one scored simulation, and its built-in Concept Recap explicitly maps every day's event back to a concept, echoing this lesson's synthesis goal. The homework workbook is the "short, friendly written check — no exam pressure" the course spec calls for; use it after the game, not before.

Worked Example
Story: "A town has one bakery. Because flour is scarce and costs money, the baker must CHOOSE what to bake — that's opportunity cost. She sets a price where the bread she wants to sell matches what shoppers want to buy — that's equilibrium. When the price of flour rises, she raises bread prices a little, and shoppers barely buy less — bread has INELASTIC demand, since people still need to eat. The town government, worried some families can't afford bread, sets a price CEILING — but now the baker can't cover her costs at busy times, and bread sometimes runs out. Frustrated, she hires more staff to bake faster and cheaper — economies of scale — until the kitchen gets too crowded and costs creep back up." Walk through how EVERY sentence uses a concept from a different lesson, then ask students to spot which one is which.
Common Misconceptions
"A shift in demand and a movement along demand are the same thing." Fix: use the Misconception Clinic (slide s3) — a price change moves you ALONG the existing curve; only a non-price factor (income, tastes, population) shifts the whole curve to a new position.
"'Price rises, so demand falls' is correct economics language." Fix: model the precise phrase out loud — "price rises, so QUANTITY DEMANDED falls" — and explain that "demand" describes the whole relationship between price and quantity, not one single amount.
"Winning the Monopoly Licence in the game is only good news, with no downside for anyone." Fix: ask a student who won what happened to the OTHER stalls and their customers that day — a monopolist gains pricing power, but buyers typically lose out on price and choice, echoing Lesson 5's lemonade role play.
Think Like an Economist
Prompt for open discussion or journaling: "Think of a rule — a school rule, a family rule, a game rule — that changed how people behaved, the same way a government rule changed behaviour in Market Day. What was the rule, and what changed once it was introduced?"
Extension Activities
  • Concept scrapbook: find or draw one real-world example for each of the five lessons in this module, and label which concept each one shows.
  • Design your own Market Day rule: invent a new government rule (not used in the game) and predict how it would change stallholders' prices and customers' choices.
  • Family economist interview: ask a family member to describe a "market" they take part in (their job, the grocery store, a hobby marketplace) and identify which of this module's ideas show up in it.

    Kid-Friendly Glossary — Module Recap
    Opportunity costThe next best alternative you give up when you make a choice, because resources are scarce.
    Equilibrium priceThe price where the amount buyers want to buy matches the amount sellers want to sell.
    ElasticityHow much the quantity bought changes when price changes — a lot (elastic) or a little (inelastic).
    Price ceiling / floorA legal maximum (ceiling) or minimum (floor) price set by the government.
    Economies of scaleWhen growing bigger makes each item cheaper to produce — up to a point.
    MonopolyA market with only ONE seller, who has more power over price than in a competitive market.
    Key Takeaways
    1. Every concept in this module connects to the same underlying story: scarce resources force choices, choices meet in markets, prices respond by degree (elasticity), and both governments and firms can reshape how that market behaves. 2. A price change moves you ALONG a demand curve; only a non-price factor SHIFTS the whole curve. 3. Cost and price are different things — cost is what a firm pays, price is what a buyer pays. 4. Government rules (taxes, subsidies, ceilings, floors) each change a market in a different, predictable direction. 5. A monopoly changes the balance of power between sellers and buyers — which is exactly what the Tier 2 track will formalise next with diagrams and exam-style reasoning.
