This lesson formalises the intuition Tier 1 students built through play (or, for IB-track students meeting this cold, builds it directly through diagrams) into the load-bearing analytical tool of the entire course: the demand/supply diagram. Every later Module 1 lesson — elasticity, government intervention, market failure — is a variation played on this same diagram. Students who leave this lesson shaky on shift-vs-movement will struggle with every subsequent diagram-based question, so treat diagram fluency here as non-negotiable, not "nice to have."
Detailed Teaching Notes
Diagram discipline from the first slide
Open with the same self-adjusting-price hypothesis Tier 1 explores through play ("a price can move without anyone deciding to move it") but move immediately into precision: run the command-term drill (slide s1) on the word "demand" across Define / State / Describe, reinforcing the technique from M1L1 that answer length and depth track the command word, not personal preference.
The full D/S diagram (slide s2) is this lesson's load-bearing skill. Insist on the exam-standard convention from the first sketch: Price on the vertical axis, Quantity on the horizontal axis, D sloping down-right, S sloping up-right, equilibrium labelled E with dashed drop-lines to Pe and Qe. Use the three-mode toggle (Original / Movement Along D / Shift of D) explicitly to drill the single most examined distinction in this topic: a movement is caused ONLY by a change in the good's own price (same curve, different point); a shift is caused by anything else — the six standard determinants covered in slide s3.
Run the full determinant taxonomy (slide s3) as a memorised, exam-ready list — unlike Tier 1, which deliberately avoids naming an exhaustive list, Tier 2 students are expected to name specific determinants in exam answers. Demand: income, tastes/fashion, price of substitutes, price of complements, expectations, population. Supply: cost of production, technology, tax/subsidy, number of sellers, price of other goods (production substitutes), expectations.
Schedules and curves together (slide s4): make students practise reading equilibrium off a numeric table as fluently as off a graph — Paper 1 MCQs frequently present a schedule rather than a drawn diagram, and students who only recognise equilibrium visually lose easy marks.
Consumer and producer surplus (slide s5) is explicitly IB SL/HL enrichment, not IGCSE 0455 core — flag this openly. It is, however, an excellent bridge into IB's welfare-analysis unit later, so it's worth teaching to all students even on the IGCSE track, framed as "this is where the model goes next if you continue into IB."
Behavioural economics (slide s6 — anchoring, herding, present bias) is tagged IB HL 2.4 enrichment. Use it as a genuine challenge to the standard model, not a footnote: ask students directly "does every buyer really have an independent, fixed willingness to pay?" and let the sneaker-hype/concert-resale examples from Tier 1's own lesson do the intuitive work before naming the formal bias terms.
Close with the cause→effect chain writing exercise (slide s7, drought → wheat harvest → bread market): this is direct rehearsal for the IB "explain" command term, which examiners score on a coherent causal sequence, not just a correct final answer. Insist students write in full linked sentences ("...which causes...", "...as a result...") rather than a bullet list.
Visuals & Diagrams
📈
D/S diagram: straight lines are acceptable and standard here (unlike the PPC's bowed curve) — D downward-sloping, S upward-sloping, both labelled, equilibrium E with Pe/Qe dashed to both axes. When drawing a shift, always add the NEW curve as a second, clearly labelled line (D₁→D₂) rather than erasing the original — examiners want to see both the before and after.
🔺
Consumer/producer surplus [IB]: consumer surplus is the triangular area between the demand curve and the horizontal price line, up to Qe; producer surplus is the triangular area between the supply curve and the price line, up to Qe. Shade each with a distinct colour/pattern and label clearly — this diagram is frequently required as a standalone IB task.
Tip: when a student's diagram shows the wrong curve shifting (e.g. shifting supply for an income change), don't correct it directly — ask "whose decision changed: the buyer's or the seller's?" and let them re-derive which curve moves.
Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "A severe drought destroys much of a country's wheat harvest. Explain the effect on the market for bread." Model a full AO2 chain: (1) identify the shock as affecting a production input, not bread's own price; (2) name the correct curve — supply of bread shifts left (higher production costs / lower input availability); (3) trace the consequence — at the old price there is now a shortage, so price rises and quantity falls to a new equilibrium; (4) note this is a shift, not a movement, because the underlying cause is external to bread's own price.
Common Misconceptions
"Any change in demand or supply is a 'shift.'" Fix: drill the single-question test — "Did the good's OWN price change? If yes, movement. If no, shift."
"A shortage means the price is too low; the fix is for the government to raise it." Fix: in a free market, no one raises it by decree — buyers bidding for scarce stock and sellers noticing they can charge more is what raises the price. Reserve "government sets the price" language for the market-failure/intervention lesson (M1L4), where it is genuinely relevant.
"Consumer surplus and profit are the same thing." Fix: consumer surplus is a buyer-side welfare gain (what they saved vs. their maximum willingness to pay); it says nothing about the seller's costs or profit, which is a separate, producer-side concept (producer surplus, and ultimately profit after costs).

Skill focus this lesson: D/S diagram — shifts vs movements; schedules AND curves; equilibrium adjustment sequence. Command terms in play: draw, label, outline, explain.
IGCSE (AO1/AO2)
Paper 1 MCQ training: single-best-answer discipline under time pressure; diagram-reading in an MCQ context (reading a shift or a movement straight off a small diagram without redrawing it).
Practice move: time-box a set of 8–10 diagram-based MCQs to 30 seconds each; debrief the ones students got wrong by identifying whether they misread axis direction or misjudged shift vs. movement.
IB (AO2)
"Explain" paragraph structure: claim → mechanism (which curve, why) → consequence (new P and Q) → link back to the question, in full linked sentences.
Practice move: take any IGCSE-style one-line answer from this lesson and extend it into a 3–4 sentence IB "explain" paragraph using the claim→mechanism→consequence structure.

Extension Activities
  • Real-market news hunt: find a real news story about a price change and classify the cause as a demand shift, a supply shift, or a movement along a curve, with justification.
  • Elasticity preview: ask why a drought might swing wheat/bread prices sharply while a drought has little effect on, say, bottled water prices — a hook into M1L3.
  • Behavioural case study: research one real product (sneaker drop, concert resale, cryptocurrency) and identify which behavioural bias (anchoring, herding, present bias) best explains observed buyer behaviour.

    Exam-Precise Glossary
    Law of demandAs price falls, quantity demanded rises, ceteris paribus, and vice versa.
    Law of supplyAs price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus, and vice versa.
    Equilibrium priceThe price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied; no tendency for price to change.
    Movement along a curveA change in quantity demanded/supplied caused only by a change in the good's own price.
    Shift of a curveA change in demand/supply at every price, caused by a non-price determinant.
    Shortage (excess demand)Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at a given price; puts upward pressure on price.
    Surplus (excess supply)Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at a given price; puts downward pressure on price.
    Consumer surplus[IB enrichment] The difference between what a consumer is willing and able to pay and what they actually pay.
    Producer surplus[IB enrichment] The difference between what a producer is willing to accept and what they actually receive.
    Behavioural bias (anchoring/herding/present bias)[IB HL 2.4 enrichment] Systematic deviations from the standard rational, independent-willingness-to-pay assumption behind demand.
    Key Takeaways
    1. The D/S diagram is the load-bearing tool of the whole course — fluency here pays off in every later lesson. 2. The own-price test cleanly separates a movement (own price changed) from a shift (something else changed). 3. Equilibrium is self-correcting: shortages push price up, surpluses push price down, with no central planner. 4. Consumer/producer surplus and behavioural economics are genuine IB enrichment — flag them as "beyond IGCSE" but valuable bridges. 5. The cause→effect chain is direct rehearsal for the IB "explain" command term: claim → mechanism → consequence.
