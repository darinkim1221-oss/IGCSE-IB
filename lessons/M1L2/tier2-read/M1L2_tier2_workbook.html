Extended Response · exam-style
10. "A shift in demand and a movement along the demand curve look similar on a diagram but mean very different things." Explain the difference between these two concepts, using ONE real-world example for each. [6]
Answer Key & Mark Scheme Notes
1. As price falls, quantity demanded rises, ceteris paribus (all else held constant) — and vice versa. [1 mark for a complete definition including the "ceteris paribus" qualifier or equivalent]
2. Equilibrium price: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price to change. [1 mark definition] + [3 marks for explaining the self-correcting mechanism: at any other price, a shortage (price too low) causes buyers to bid price up, or a surplus (price too high) causes unsold stock/sellers to cut price — no single actor plans this, it emerges from many individual reactions to shortages/surpluses]
3. Income rises → Demand, shifts right (normal good). Raw material cost rises → Supply, shifts left. New subsidy → Supply, shifts right. Becomes fashionable → Demand, shifts right. [1 mark per correct curve ×4, 1 mark per correct direction ×4]
4. Downward-sloping D curve, upward-sloping S curve, both correctly labelled, intersecting at E with dashed lines down/across to Pe and Qe on the axes. [1 mark each for correct D slope, correct S slope, correctly marked E, and correctly dropped Pe/Qe lines]
5. This is a shift of the SUPPLY curve to the LEFT (less wheat available at every price due to the drought reducing the raw input, not because bread's own price changed). The new equilibrium has a higher price and lower quantity than before. [1 mark shift not movement, 1 mark correct curve (supply), 1 mark correct direction (left), 2 marks for correctly reasoned effect on new P and Q]
6. Equilibrium: P=$6, Q=75 (the only row where Qd is close to/equal Qs — accept the row where they are closest if the schedule doesn't intersect exactly, but here Qd=75/Qs=45 at $6 does not match exactly; correct reading is that equilibrium lies between $6 and $8, closer to where the lines cross around P≈$7). At $8: quantity supplied (70) exceeds quantity demanded (50), creating a SURPLUS of 20 units, which puts downward pressure on price. [2 marks for identifying the surplus at $8 and its size, 2 marks for correctly describing the downward pressure it creates — accept reasonable interpolation for exact equilibrium given the discrete schedule]
7. Consumer surplus: the difference between what a consumer is willing and able to pay and what they actually pay (area below the demand curve, above price). Producer surplus: the difference between what a producer is willing to accept and what they actually receive (area above the supply curve, below price). When equilibrium price falls, consumer surplus increases — consumers keep paying less than their maximum willingness to pay across a larger quantity. [1 mark each definition, 2 marks for correctly explaining the increase in CS with reasoning, not just the conclusion]
8. The standard demand curve assumes each buyer's willingness to pay is independent and based on their own private valuation of the good. Herding means one person's demand is influenced by other people's demand/purchases, which can cause demand (and willingness to pay) to rise as popularity rises — the opposite of what independent, price-driven demand would predict, and a challenge to the ceteris paribus assumption underlying the standard curve. [1 mark identifying the independence assumption, 2 marks for explaining how herding breaks it]
9. A shortage (excess demand) — quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at $40. Likely consumer response: queuing/camping for tickets, buying via resale/secondary markets at a much higher price, or repeat refreshing of a ticket website ("digital queuing"). [1 mark shortage correctly identified, 2 marks for a specific, well-explained real consumer response]
10. Band 3 (5–6 marks) answers should: correctly state that a movement along the demand curve is caused ONLY by a change in the good's OWN price (e.g. a sale drops the price of trainers, so quantity demanded of those trainers rises — same curve, different point); a shift of the demand curve is caused by a change in a non-price determinant (e.g. incomes rise, so demand for trainers increases at every price — a whole new curve). A strong answer explicitly notes both examples and states the causal rule ("own price → movement; anything else → shift") rather than just describing what each looks like on a graph. Band 2 (3–4): one concept explained well with example, the other underdeveloped or missing its own example. Band 1 (1–2): describes the diagrams visually without the causal own-price-vs-determinant rule.
