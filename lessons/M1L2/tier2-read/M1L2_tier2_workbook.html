Answer Key & Mark Scheme Notes
1. As price falls, quantity demanded rises, ceteris paribus (all else held constant) — and vice versa. [1 mark for a complete definition including the "ceteris paribus" qualifier or equivalent]
2. Equilibrium price: the price at which quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so there is no tendency for price to change. [1 mark definition] + [3 marks for explaining the self-correcting mechanism: at any other price, a shortage (price too low) causes buyers to bid price up, or a surplus (price too high) causes unsold stock/sellers to cut price — no single actor plans this, it emerges from many individual reactions to shortages/surpluses]
3. Downward-sloping D curve, upward-sloping S curve, both correctly labelled, intersecting at E with dashed lines down/across to Pe and Qe on the axes. [1 mark each for correct D slope, correct S slope, correctly marked E, and correctly dropped Pe/Qe lines]
4. A shortage (excess demand) — quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at $40. Likely consumer response: queuing/camping for tickets, buying via resale/secondary markets at a much higher price, or repeat refreshing of a ticket website ("digital queuing"). [1 mark shortage correctly identified, 2 marks for a specific, well-explained real consumer response]
5. Band 3 (5–6 marks) answers should: correctly state that a movement along the demand curve is caused ONLY by a change in the good's OWN price (e.g. a sale drops the price of trainers, so quantity demanded of those trainers rises — same curve, different point); a shift of the demand curve is caused by a change in a non-price determinant (e.g. incomes rise, so demand for trainers increases at every price — a whole new curve). A strong answer explicitly notes both examples and states the causal rule ("own price → movement; anything else → shift") rather than just describing what each looks like on a graph. Band 2 (3–4): one concept explained well with example, the other underdeveloped or missing its own example. Band 1 (1–2): describes the diagrams visually without the causal own-price-vs-determinant rule.
