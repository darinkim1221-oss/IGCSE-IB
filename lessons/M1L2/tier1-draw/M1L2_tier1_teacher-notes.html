Learning Objectives
By the end, I can…
explain why more people want to buy something when its price falls
explain why sellers want to sell more when the price rises
spot a shortage (too many buyers) and a surplus (too much stock) and know which way price should move to fix each
name things other than price that change how much I want to buy
describe how a "nudge" can change a choice without banning anything

This lesson is the first time students see a self-correcting system with no one in charge — genuinely one of the more surprising ideas in the whole course for this age group. Protect the "aha" moment: let the live pit market and Market Rush game do the discovery work before naming anything "the price mechanism." The revenue link ("what happens to sales when price changes") is deliberately left as a felt experience here — it gets formalised at M1L3 (Elasticity).
Detailed Teaching Notes
Discovery through a live market, not a lecture
Open with the sneaker-drop hook (500 buyers, 50 pairs) and let students guess what happens without giving away vocabulary. Then run the Live Pit Market: split the class into buyers and sellers, hand out secret price cards (max price for buyers, min price for sellers), and let them wander and try deals for 5–6 minutes. Do NOT explain "equilibrium" beforehand — let the class's own trades cluster around a price naturally. The digital Pit Market slide (s1) is a good warm-up or backup if a live version isn't practical that day.
Immediately after, plot the class's actual trade prices as a simple table/curve (slide s2) — this is deliberately called "your class's first demand curve," not introduced as an abstract diagram. The point that matters at Tier 1: lower price, more buyers show up.
For demand shifters (slide s3), resist listing the formal taxonomy (income, tastes, substitutes, complements, expectations, population) — Tier 1's job is just "what would make YOU want more of this, even at the same price?" The taxonomy is a Tier 2 promotion.
The shortage/surplus slider (concert tickets) and the Market Rush game both let students discover the adjustment mechanism through repeated trial rather than being told the rule — assign Market Rush as the follow-up activity or homework kickoff if class time is short, since it reinforces the exact same mechanic solo.
Close with the nudge bonus — keep it light and concrete (eye-level shelf placement). This plants a seed for behavioural economics without naming any formal bias.
Visuals & Diagrams
📊
Class demand curve: on the board, plot each round's (price, number of buyers willing) as a dot — connect them freehand. Don't worry about a perfectly smooth curve; the downward trend is the whole lesson.
⚖️
Shortage vs. surplus: sketch two simple scale icons — one tipped toward "buyers" (shortage, price should rise) and one tipped toward "stock" (surplus, price should fall). Keep it visual, not a formal D/S diagram (that's the Tier 2 promotion).
Tip: if a student asks "who actually changes the price then?", the honest Tier 1 answer is: usually the seller, reacting to how fast things sell or how much is left unsold — no single "market boss" exists.
Worked Example
Story: "A food truck sells burritos for $8 and sells out in 20 minutes every day, with people still in line when they close." Ask: shortage or surplus? (Shortage.) What should the price probably do next week? (Rise — until the line and the stock roughly match.)
Common Misconceptions
"A shop should always charge the highest price possible." Fix: show the surplus case — pairs sit unsold on the shelf at too-high a price, earning nothing.
"Prices are set by the government / one boss." Fix: point back to the live pit market — dozens of individual trades, with no single person setting the price, still produced a converging price.
"If people want something, sellers will just make more no matter what." Fix: revisit the supply-side mini game — sellers respond to price and cost, not just want.
Think Like an Economist (4 min, replaces the exam-adapter slot at Tier 1)
Close with: What did we discover today? (prices move on their own, driven by shortages and surpluses) → Where else does this happen? (ask for an example — concert tickets, a school bake sale running out, holiday-season discounts on unsold stock).
Extension Activities
  • Price-watch mission: notice one thing this week that changed price — was it a shortage, a surplus, or something else (like a sale)?
  • Mini debate: "Should concert tickets be sold to whoever pays the most, or first-come-first-served?" — no research needed, 2 min per side.
  • Family mission: the workbook's take-home shortage/surplus interview.

    Child-Friendly Glossary
    DemandHow much of something people want to buy at a given price.
    SupplyHow much of something sellers are willing to offer at a given price.
    ShortageWhen more people want to buy than there is stock available.
    SurplusWhen there's more stock than people want to buy.
    Price mechanismThe way prices rise and fall on their own to fix shortages and surpluses.
    NudgeA small design choice that encourages a choice without banning any option.
    Key Takeaways
    1. Lower price → more buyers; higher price → more sellers willing to supply. 2. A shortage pushes price up; a surplus pushes price down — no one needs to be "in charge." 3. Things besides price (trends, friends, money, weather) also change how much people want. 4. Small nudges can shape choices without banning anything — a first taste of behavioural economics.
