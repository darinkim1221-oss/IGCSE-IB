🧠 For Tutors — Game Guide & Debrief
What this game teaches
The price mechanism, felt as a search rather than told as a fact. The student sets a price for sneakers and immediately sees two numbers react: the queue (buyers who want in at that price) and the shelf (pairs the stall is willing to stock at that price). There is no "correct" starting price — the game is won by noticing the pattern (raise price → queue shrinks, shelf grows; lower price → the reverse) and using it to hunt for the point where they match: the equilibrium.
Core mechanic
Each round: set the price (Raise $5 / Lower $5 / Hold), see the new queue and shelf numbers, get a Shortage / Surplus / Balanced verdict. Six rounds to reach Balanced. This is literally the same adjustment process as the "live pit market" classroom version — the game gives every student their own market to run individually first.
Round structure
  • Briefing: the sneaker-drop scenario is set — starting price $60, way too many buyers.
  • Rounds 1–6: student picks Raise / Lower / Hold each round; queue & shelf update live with a verdict banner.
  • Result: shows how many rounds it took to reach Balanced (or the closest they got), plus a score tier.
Pre-game brief (1 minute)
"You run a sneaker stall. Right now the price is too low — everyone wants a pair and there's a huge queue. You control ONE thing: the price. Watch what happens to the queue and the shelf each time you move it, and try to find the price where nobody's left waiting and nothing's left unsold."
Debrief questions
  • What happened to the queue every time you raised the price? What happened to the shelf?
  • Nobody told you the "correct" price — how did you find it anyway?
  • In real life, who actually moves the price like you just did — a boss, the government, or something else?
Common misconceptions & fix
"Someone in charge sets the 'right' price." Point out that the student had no target number given — they found it purely by watching queue vs. shelf react, which is what real markets do without anyone in charge. "Higher price is always better for the seller." Show the surplus rounds — pairs sit unsold and unsold stock earns nothing.
Variants
Simpler: reveal the exact queue/shelf numbers needed to balance before round 1. Harder: add a surprise "shifter" event mid-game (e.g. "a celebrity just wore these!" — queue jumps) and have students re-find the new balance point.
