Detailed Teaching Notes
Full syllabus rigor — the diagram, the calculations, the policy debate
  • Command-term drill: run Define / Explain / Comment first — "explain" previews the diagram-shift reasoning needed next.
  • Currency D/S diagram: drill the sequence every time — identify WHICH curve shifts (demand or supply), in WHICH direction, THEN state the resulting appreciation or depreciation. The toggle buttons let students compare all cases side by side.
  • Reasons & causes tabs: nine tabs combining the six reasons currencies are traded with the three named causes of fluctuation (visually flagged with ⚡) — treat the causes as the "so what" of knowing the reasons.
  • Cross-currency conversion: drill formula → substitution → labelled answer every time, exactly like other IGCSE calculation skills this course has built.
  • Current account calculator: stress that a NEGATIVE total is a deficit, a POSITIVE total is a surplus — a common error is mislabeling the sign.
  • IB HL enrichment: flag clearly that fixed/managed exchange rate systems and the financial account are NOT required at IGCSE 0455 — floating rates only are examined there.

The currency demand-and-supply diagram is the key visual for this lesson — treat the currency exactly like any other traded good, with "Exchange Rate" on the price axis. Worked Example
Exam-style prompt: "Discuss whether a government should intervene to reduce a persistent current account deficit." Model: CLAIM — intervention can help but carries real trade-offs. EVIDENCE — raising interest rates to reduce import spending could narrow the deficit, but higher rates also risk higher unemployment and slower growth; alternatively, supply-side policies to boost export competitiveness avoid this trade-off but take much longer to work. LINK — a persistent, large deficit may justify some intervention, but a small or temporary deficit funding productive investment may not be worth the trade-off of slower growth. This is the exact structure required in workbook Q6.
Common Misconceptions
"A stronger currency is always better for a country." Fix: use the Consequences tabs — exporters generally prefer a WEAKER currency, since it makes them more price-competitive abroad.
"Appreciation means the government raised the exchange rate." Fix: appreciation describes a FLOATING currency's value rising due to market forces (demand/supply), not a government-set change — that would be a revaluation under a fixed system (IB HL only).
"A current account deficit means the country is in debt." Fix: a deficit is a FLOW for one year (more money out than in); it can contribute to the country's overall net asset position over time, but is not itself "debt" in the way a loan is.

Exam-Precise Glossary
AppreciationA rise in the value of a floating currency relative to other currencies.
DepreciationA fall in the value of a floating currency relative to other currencies.
Current accountTrade in goods + trade in services + primary income + secondary income.
Current account deficitMore money flowing out of the current account than in.
Current account surplusMore money flowing into the current account than out.
Financial account (IB)Records investment capital flows (e.g. FDI) between countries — separate from the current account.
